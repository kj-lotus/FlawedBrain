## Chapter 9

- **Groups can be categorized into different types**, such as formal groups (created by organizations to achieve specific goals) and informal groups (formed naturally by individuals for social purposes).
- **The punctuated equilibrium model describes group development** as alternating periods of stability and rapid change, with groups experiencing inertia, a mid-point transition, and then accelerated activity toward task completion.
- **Role requirements vary depending on the situation**, meaning individuals may adapt their behavior and responsibilities based on group context and expectations.
- **Norms strongly influence individual behavior**, as group-established standards guide members’ actions and can lead to conformity.
- **Status differences within a group affect performance**, with higher-status members often having more influence, but status disparities can also create tension or reduce participation from lower-status members.
- **Group size impacts performance**, with smaller groups typically being more efficient and larger groups offering greater diversity but facing challenges like coordination and social loafing.
- **Cohesiveness and diversity both contribute to group effectiveness**, as cohesive groups are more unified and motivated, while diverse groups bring varied perspectives and skills; integrating both can enhance outcomes.
- **Group decision making has strengths and weaknesses**, including benefits like pooling knowledge and generating creative solutions, but also drawbacks such as slower processes, potential for groupthink, and difficulty reaching consensus.

## The DART Mission: A Lesson in High-stakes Group Decision Making

- **The DART mission demonstrated the importance of group norms and collaborative problem-solving in high-stakes, uncertain situations.** When a critical risk was identified in the navigation system, leadership chose to address concerns through a cross-functional task group, leading to a successful mission outcome.
- **Group norms—such as open critique, collaborative resilience, and dynamic role allocation—were essential for effective teamwork.** Status was earned through clear communication and negotiation, not just by title, and all voices, including those of lower-ranking members, were valued.
- **Tension and disagreement within groups can drive better solutions if managed constructively.** The DART team used friction between scientific and engineering subteams to fuel sharper problem-solving rather than allowing it to fracture the group.
- **Groups and teams are distinct: all teams are groups, but not all groups are teams.** Teams require collaboration, coordination, and shared accountability to achieve common goals, while groups may not need such interdependence.
- **Belonging to a group influences individual perceptions and behaviors.** Understanding how groups form, evolve, and function is crucial for navigating complex organizational challenges and is foundational to the study of organizational behavior.

## Defining and Classifying Groups

- **A group consists of two or more individuals who interact and depend on each other to achieve specific objectives.**
- **Groups can be classified as formal or informal:**
    - **Formal groups** are defined by an organization’s structure, have designated work assignments, and focus on organizational goals (e.g., an airline flight crew).
    - **Informal groups** are not officially structured or determined by the organization and typically form to satisfy social needs or shared interests (e.g., employees from different departments who regularly have lunch together).
- **Individuals often belong to multiple groups simultaneously, and the importance or influence of each group can change over time; sometimes, roles in different groups may conflict (such as being both a parent and a manager).**
- **Both formal and informal groups significantly influence individual behavior and performance within organizations.**

## Social Identity

- **Social identity theory explains why you emotionally invest in your groups**—your self-esteem becomes linked to the group’s successes and failures, leading to pride, anger, or even pleasure at competitors’ misfortunes.
- **Shared experiences and meaningful activities, like volunteering, strengthen group ties and well-being**; employees who volunteer together report better health, less depression, and higher engagement, as these activities increase identification with their employer and coworkers.
- **You develop multiple social identities throughout life**—such as profession, nationality, religion, or gender—and the importance of each identity can shift depending on context and experience, sometimes leading to identity conflict or transformation.
- **Relational identification (connecting through roles) and collective identification (connecting through group characteristics) both shape your workplace identity**; typically, you identify more strongly with your immediate group or team than with the broader organization, but both forms of identification are important for positive attitudes and behaviors.
- **Low group identification can decrease satisfaction and engagement**, and is linked to fewer organizational citizenship behaviors; for example, immigrants with unsupportive supervisors or weak community ties often experience threatened identities and higher turnover.
- **Language used by leaders (e.g., “we” or “us” vs. “I”) signals group belonging**; studies show that collective language from CEOs correlates with better organizational performance, such as increased sales and ROI.
- **Social categorization leads to distinguishing between ingroups and outgroups**, which can foster prejudice, discrimination, and polarization; people tend to favor their own group, sometimes resulting in discrimination through favoritism rather than hostility.
- **Remote employees, especially women, are often seen as outgroup members and face bias in performance reviews**, with skepticism about their productivity compared to men, highlighting how group dynamics can perpetuate inequality.

## Stages of Group Development

- **The punctuated equilibrium model describes how temporary groups with deadlines progress through distinct phases:** The group sets its direction at the first meeting, followed by a period of inertia where little progress is made.
- **A critical transition occurs at the midpoint of the group's timeline:** This transition acts as a wake-up call, prompting significant changes, dropping old patterns, and adopting new approaches to meet the deadline.
- **After the transition, a second phase of inertia follows:** The group implements plans made during the transition, leading up to a final burst of activity at the last meeting to complete the task.
- **The model is especially relevant for temporary task groups with clear deadlines:** It emphasizes the impact of time constraints on group behavior and productivity.
- **The Tuckman model offers an alternative perspective:** It outlines stages of forming, storming, norming, and performing, which may overlap with the phases of the punctuated equilibrium model, but lacks strong research support despite its popularity.
- **Key group properties such as roles, norms, status, size, and cohesion are also crucial for understanding group dynamics in organizations.**

## Group Property 1: Roles

- **Role requirements change depending on the group and situation you are in**, meaning you may be expected to behave differently as a partner, parent, professional, or in other roles.
- **Different groups have different expectations for their members**, and while some roles can be compatible, others may lead to conflicts, such as balancing family and career demands.
- **You use your own perceptions of roles to guide your behavior and to interpret what is expected of you by others**, helping you understand and meet group expectations.
- **Understanding the boundaries and expectations of your roles helps you reduce role conflict**, making it easier to manage multiple responsibilities.

## Role Perception

- **Role perception refers to your understanding of how you are expected to behave in a specific situation.**
- You develop role perceptions from various sources around you, such as family, coworkers, and media.
- Some role perceptions can transfer between different areas of your life; for instance, leadership skills from family roles may positively influence your work roles.
- However, certain role perceptions are context-specific and do not transfer; for example, U.S. Marines may keep their work roles separate from their family lives, deliberately preventing overlap.

## Role Expectations

- **Role expectations are shaped by how others believe you should act in a specific context**, influencing your own perception of your role; for example, a supervisor’s expectations for innovation can lead you to view creativity as part of your job.
- **The psychological contract is an unwritten agreement between employees and employers that sets mutual expectations**, such as fair treatment, clear communication, and feedback from management, and good attitude, loyalty, and compliance from employees.
- **Breaches of the psychological contract can lead to negative outcomes like reduced performance, counterproductive work behavior, turnover, and dissatisfaction**, while fulfillment increases gratitude, organizational identification, and willingness to perform extra-role behaviors; trust can be repaired after a breach through forgiveness and taking responsibility.

## Role Conflict

- **Role conflict arises when fulfilling the expectations of one role makes it difficult to meet the demands of another role.** For example, professors may struggle to excel as both teachers and researchers due to limited time.
- **Interrole conflict occurs when the expectations of different groups or roles a person belongs to are in opposition.** This is common for individuals holding multiple jobs or balancing work and family responsibilities, such as a parent managing recovery from childbirth while assembling leave to care for a newborn.
- **Role conflict is a significant source of stress for employees.** Examples include nurses providing professional care to loved ones during disasters, or employees experiencing identity conflict during mergers, acquisitions, or in multinational organizations.
- **Some individuals cope with role conflict through creative strategies and adopting a mindfulness mindset.** Managerial support is especially important for new employees facing competing demands, helping them balance job responsibilities and learning new roles.

## Myth or Science?

- **Gossip is not always toxic and can serve positive social functions**; prosocial gossip can expose exploitative behavior, leading to positive changes such as discouraging bullying and promoting cooperation within groups.
- **Group norms influence the effects of gossip**; positive norms can enhance beneficial gossip and discourage harmful forms, while negative norms can have the opposite effect.
- **Gossip can encourage conformity to group norms**; individuals who are the subject of gossip may change their behavior to align with group expectations, and others may also cooperate more to avoid negative attention.
- **Exclusion in the workplace takes two forms: leaving someone out and ostracism**; ostracism is a more direct and felt punishment, often leading to increased cooperation from the excluded individual as they attempt to regain group acceptance.
- **The context of ostracism matters**; while it can motivate conformity, it can also lower self-esteem and lead to disengagement if not managed carefully.
- **Gossip and ostracism together can increase cooperation**; research shows that when both are possible, individuals are more likely to cooperate with the group.
- **Positive effects of gossip and exclusion depend on certain conditions**: the gossip should be truthful and work-related, the excluded individual should have the opportunity to rejoin the group, and group norms should be constructive.
- **Non-work-related gossip does not provide value to groups** and should be avoided.

## Group Property 2: Norms

- **Norms are shared standards of behavior** within a group that define what you should and should not do in specific situations.
- **Norms vary across different groups and settings**, explaining why behaviors like wearing shoes indoors are acceptable in some places (e.g., Best Buy) but not in others (e.g., Gusto’s office or certain homes).
- **Norms are not simply rules set by leaders**; they must be accepted and internalized by group members to be effective and lasting.
- **Norms influence individual behavior** by shaping emotional responses, guiding actions, and creating expectations for how you and others should act in various environments.

## Norms and Emotions

- **Group emotions and norms are closely linked**, with shared emotions among group members reinforcing the power of group norms and influencing how individuals interpret and express their feelings.
- **Norms can shape moral emotions**, leading group members to respond to ethical situations in ways that align with group expectations, such as showing compassion, condemning unethical actions, or attempting to correct wrongs.
- **Hybrid and remote work environments introduce new norms**, often resulting in less self-monitoring and self-control during digital communication, which can increase the likelihood of expressing negative or disrespectful emotions.
- **Remote work norms do not directly cause workplace toxicity**, but they can make it easier for negative behaviors, such as incivility or abuse, to occur due to reduced face-to-face interaction and oversight.
- **A lack of connection in remote settings can foster negative emotions**, including diminished trust among employees.

## Norms and Conformity

- **Groups exert strong pressure for conformity**, leading you to align your behavior and judgments with group norms, especially when you perceive others are doing the same.
- **Solomon Asch’s experiments demonstrated the power of group influence**: when faced with unanimous but obviously incorrect group answers, 75% of participants conformed at least once, and on average, participants conformed 37% of the time, even when they knew the group was wrong.
- **Conformity is not absolute**—you do not always yield to group pressure, and most people prefer independence over conformity, especially when the pressure feels uncomfortable.
- **You are most likely to conform to your reference groups**, which are groups you identify with, aspire to join, or consider important; these groups have the greatest influence on your behavior.
- **Conformity can have positive outcomes**, such as encouraging prosocial behaviors like generosity and empathy, not just negative or mindless agreement.

## Norms and Behavior

- **Norms strongly influence group and employee behavior**, often more than physical conditions or incentives.
- The Hawthorne Studies revealed that **increased attention and perceived special status** led to higher productivity, not just changes in the physical environment like lighting.
- **Group dynamics and social factors** were found to be more impactful than environmental changes; productivity increased even when lighting was reduced, showing the power of group influence.
- When workers felt **valued and observed**, their performance improved and absenteeism decreased, as seen in the small group of women assembling telephones.
- **Group norms can override individual incentives**; in the bank wiring room, employees limited their output to avoid negative consequences for the group, such as rate cuts or layoffs.
- The group established and enforced specific behavioral norms, such as not working too fast ("rate-buster"), not working too slow ("chiseler"), and not reporting peers, using social pressure and even mild physical enforcement.
- As a result, **group norms led to performance below actual capability**, demonstrating the powerful role of social expectations and peer enforcement in workplace behavior.

## Norms and Psychological Safety

- **Psychological safety is a group norm defined by a shared belief that the team is a safe space for interpersonal risk taking**, allowing you to express ideas, ask questions, voice concerns, challenge opinions, and admit mistakes without fear of negative consequences.
- **Psychological safety is not about being "nice" or avoiding conflict**, but about fostering an environment where open communication, productive disagreement, and learning from errors are encouraged.
- **Research shows that psychological safety leads to higher engagement, stronger learning behaviors, and better group performance**, as seen in studies like Google's "Project Aristotle," which identified psychological safety as the most critical factor for high-performing teams.
- **Groups with psychological safety promote information sharing, creative thinking, and proactive problem solving**, which build collective self-efficacy and collaborative learning, enhancing overall effectiveness.
- **A lack of psychological safety results in withheld information, reduced creativity, and increased conformity pressures**, raising the risk of groupthink.
- **Psychological safety is particularly vital in hybrid or remote teams**, where isolation and lack of visibility can make it harder for members to speak up; leaders and members must actively create opportunities for safe communication and model vulnerability.
- **Consistently reinforcing behaviors that support psychological safety helps groups adapt, learn, and achieve superior results**, especially in complex or uncertain environments.

## Positive Norms and Group Outcomes

- **Positive group norms can lead to positive outcomes, but only when supported by other factors**; for example, strong cooperation and punishment norms often develop in groups facing high threats, which helps explain cultural differences in norms.
- **Organizational values and norms, such as those promoted through corporate social responsibility (CSR), are more likely to influence employees when employees are satisfied with their group**, leading to stronger alignment and greater positive impact.
- **Norms that seem positive may have unintended negative effects**; for instance, employees may avoid using family-friendly benefits if they perceive their peers do not use them, and efforts to raise awareness about stereotyping can sometimes increase stereotype-consistent behaviors.

## Negative Norms and Group Outcomes

- **Unethical behavior in the workplace refers to voluntary actions that violate moral norms and threaten the well-being of the organization or its members.** This behavior is also called deviance and is a form of counterproductive work behavior (CWB).
- **Deviant workplace behaviors are categorized into four main types:**
    - Production deviance (e.g., leaving early, intentionally working slowly, wasting resources)
    - Property deviance (e.g., sabotage, lying about hours worked, stealing from the organization)
    - Political deviance (e.g., showing favoritism, gossiping, blaming coworkers)
    - Personal aggression (e.g., sexual harassment, verbal abuse, stealing from coworkers)
- **Negative group norms and leadership behaviors can facilitate deviant behavior.** For example, frequent absences by team members or supervisors can set a precedent, leading others to also be absent.
- **Three main reasons why deviant behaviors persist in organizations:**
    - Negative group attributes can foster unethical behavior ("bad apples" come from "bad barrels").
    - Employee mistreatment by supervisors, coworkers, or customers can provoke unethical responses; uncivil workplaces double the likelihood of employee turnover.
    - Increased work demands and pressure to do more with less can indirectly encourage deviant behavior.
- **Group context and norms strongly influence antisocial actions.** Employees are more likely to engage in deviant behaviors if their peers do so, especially if they socialize with those who frequently break norms.
- **Consequences of workplace deviance for groups include a chain reaction:** Negative behaviors (like shirking or undermining coworkers) lead to negative group moods, which then result in poor coordination and lower group performance.

## Norms and Culture

- **Cultural norms differ significantly between collectivist and individualist cultures**, often making some norms invisible or misunderstood by outsiders.
- **Your cultural orientation can temporarily shift in group contexts**; for example, being primed with collectivist or individualist norms can influence your motivation depending on whether you choose a task yourself or are assigned one by an ingroup member.
- **Workplace behaviors, such as negotiation strategies, are shaped by cultural norms**; aggressive negotiation is more common in honor and face cultures (e.g., Qatar, China) than in dignity cultures (e.g., United States).
- **Despite valuing harmony, individuals in Asian cultures may adopt competitive strategies to maintain face in professional settings**, showing that context can override general cultural tendencies.
- **Reactions to norm violations differ by culture**; in collectivist cultures, violators are perceived as less powerful and provoke stronger moral outrage compared to individualist cultures.

## Group Property 3: Status

- **Status refers to the socially defined position or rank assigned to groups or group members by others, and it exists in all societies and groups, regardless of size.**
- **Groups naturally develop status hierarchies, with some members holding higher or lower status, which may or may not reflect their status outside the group.**
- **Status differences significantly influence group performance and behavior, serving as a motivator and affecting how individuals act within the group.**
- **Disparities between an individual's self-perceived status and the status assigned by others can lead to major behavioral consequences, impacting group dynamics and outcomes.**

## What Determines Status?

- **Status in groups is influenced by three main sources:** power over others, ability to contribute to group goals, and valued personal characteristics.
- **Power increases status** because those who control group resources or outcomes are seen as higher status.
- **Critical contributions to group success** elevate status, as individuals essential to achieving group goals are valued more highly.
- **Personal characteristics such as attractiveness, intelligence, wealth, or likability** can also raise an individual's status if these traits are valued by the group.

## Status and Norms

- **High-status individuals are more likely to deviate from group norms, especially when they have low identification with the group**, as they are less influenced by group expectations and more likely to prioritize personal values.
- **Social categorization theory explains that weak group identification reduces normative influence**, allowing high-status members to assert independence and challenge group conventions.
- **Examples include highly skilled professionals (like software engineers or physicians) resisting rules or decisions imposed by lower-status individuals**, often viewing such interventions as illegitimate due to status differences.
- **High-status members are better able to resist conformity pressures**, particularly if they do not value the group’s social rewards, which can lead to both improved group performance and the introduction of counterproductive norms.
- **High-status individuals may rationalize unethical behaviors as necessary or justified**, making them less likely to reject such actions on principle.
- **Informal group processes, such as gossip, serve as powerful tools for social regulation**, shaping reputations and holding even high-status members accountable to group standards by either conferring status or damaging reputations when norms are violated.

## Status and Group Interaction

- **Individuals seeking higher status in a group often become more assertive**, displaying behaviors such as speaking out more, criticizing, giving commands, using specialized jargon, doing favors, and interrupting others.
- **High-status individuals may sometimes conceal their status** to maintain group harmony, rather than asserting dominance.
- **Lower-status members tend to participate less**, which can lead to underutilization of their expertise and insights, ultimately reducing group performance.
- **Supervisors can improve group outcomes by encouraging lower-status members to contribute** more actively to discussions.
- **Groups composed entirely of high-status individuals do not necessarily perform better**; optimal group performance often occurs when there is a mix of high- and mid-status members, as too many high-status individuals can negatively impact group effectiveness.

## Status Inequity

- **Perceived equity in status hierarchies is crucial for group stability**; when members sense inequity, it creates disequilibrium and prompts corrective behaviors.
- **Large status differences within groups are linked to negative outcomes** such as poorer individual performance, lower health, increased resentment, and higher intentions for lower-status members to leave.
- **Inequity in power-based status leads to more negative effects and interpersonal conflict**, while inequity in other forms of status can foster healthy competition.
- **Groups typically agree on status criteria**, which can vary by context: business executives may use income or company growth, bureaucrats may use budget size, and blue-collar workers may use seniority.
- **Managers with central positions in social networks are perceived as higher status** and have greater influence over group functioning.
- **Accurate knowledge of the group’s status hierarchy improves individual performance and networking** within the group, regardless of one’s own status.
- **Informal status orders are based on ranking and control of resources**, and conflicts can arise when individuals move between groups with different status criteria or when groups are heterogeneous.
- **Heterogeneous groups may experience conflict as they reconcile different status hierarchies**, especially when teams are formed from varied backgrounds or functions.
- **Threats to status can provoke extreme actions from high-status members**, such as engaging in unethical behavior to maintain their position.
- **Losing high-status members to higher-status competitors can harm the group**, but if a high-status member leaves for a lower-status group, the original group may benefit.

## An Ethical Choice

- **Narcissists can bring both strengths and challenges to groups**: Their charisma, drive, and motivation to lead can help in adaptive or chaotic environments, but they often prioritize their own interests over the group’s.
- **Narcissists react negatively to low-status roles**: They tend to be less satisfied and more defensive when not given high-status positions, unlike less-narcissistic individuals.
- **Social connectedness is often self-serving for narcissists**: They seek group involvement mainly for attention or self-esteem, and are less likely to help the group through extra-role behaviors.
- **Group cohesion with narcissists is situational**: Cohesion may increase if the narcissist is admired, but rivalry or unpopularity can lead to attempts to remove rivals, ostracism, or the narcissist leaving the group.
- **Expertise can protect narcissists from ostracism**: Narcissists with high expertise are less likely to be ostracized than those with less expertise.
- **Strategies to manage narcissists include fostering group cohesion and peer feedback**: Strong group norms and constructive peer feedback (rather than criticism from a single leader) can help narcissists adapt and reduce negative impacts on the group.
- **Managing narcissists is challenging but crucial for group success**: They often do not recognize their problematic behavior, so consistent group strategies are necessary to maintain cohesion.

## Group Property 4: Size and Dynamics

- **Group size impacts performance and creativity differently:** Larger groups (12+ members) are more effective for gathering diverse input and generating ideas, while smaller groups (about 7 members) are better for performance, productivity, and transformational leadership.
- **Group size influences ethical perceptions and helping behavior:** Smaller groups are perceived as more trustworthy, and people show less compassion and are less likely to help larger groups.
- **Social loafing increases with group size:** Individuals tend to exert less effort in larger groups due to diffusion of responsibility and the belief that others are not contributing fairly, which can reduce overall group productivity.
- **Factors affecting social loafing:** Strong individual work ethic, high conscientiousness, and agreeableness in group members decrease social loafing; performance diversity and time together increase it, especially in virtual groups with dissimilar members and external distractions.
- **Strategies to reduce social loafing:** Set clear group goals, increase intergroup competition, use peer evaluations, select highly motivated members, and base rewards on identifiable individual contributions.
- **Cultural differences in group motivation:** Social loafing is more common in individualistic cultures, but in collectivist societies, group goals motivate higher performance in group settings.

## Group Property 5: Cohesion

- **Group cohesiveness** refers to the strength of the bonds and motivation among group members to remain part of the group, influenced by factors like time spent together, group size, purpose, and external threats.
- **Cohesion and productivity are interrelated**: high cohesion can boost productivity, especially when group members' roles are interdependent, but the effect depends on the group's performance-related norms.
- **Performance norms moderate the impact of cohesion**:
    - High cohesion + high performance norms = highest productivity.
    - High cohesion + low performance norms = low productivity.
    - Low cohesion + high performance norms = moderate productivity.
    - Low cohesion + low performance norms = low-to-moderate productivity.
- **Cohesion can lead to positive outcomes** such as increased organizational citizenship behaviors (OCBs) and greater job confidence among members.
- **Strategies to increase group cohesiveness** include making the group smaller, fostering agreement on group goals, increasing time spent together, raising group status and exclusivity, stimulating competition with other groups, rewarding the group as a whole, and physically isolating the group.

## Toward a Better World

- **Employee volunteering enhances group cohesion and fosters empathy**, breaking down barriers and creating a culture of collaboration and purpose within organizations.
- **Volunteering provides individual benefits** such as improved mental health, reduced stress, and increased physical health, which can positively impact the workplace environment.
- **Organizations can normalize volunteering** by offering paid time off, partnering with aligned nonprofits, organizing group events, and recognizing employee service; the impact is greater when employees are actively involved in planning and motivated by prosocial intentions.
- **Volunteering promotes inclusivity and perspective taking**, helping employees understand diverse viewpoints and reduce prejudice, especially in demographically diverse groups, which leads to improved group functioning.

## Group Decision Making

- **Group decision making leverages the collective knowledge, experience, and perspectives of multiple individuals, which can lead to higher-quality decisions compared to those made by individuals alone.**
- **Groups can generate more alternatives and creative solutions due to diverse viewpoints and collaborative brainstorming.**
- **Participation in group decisions can increase acceptance and commitment to the final outcome among group members.**
- **Group decision making can be less efficient, as it often takes more time to reach a consensus due to differing opinions and the need for discussion and negotiation.**
- **Group dynamics can introduce challenges such as groupthink, where the desire for harmony or conformity results in poor decision outcomes, and social loafing, where some members contribute less effort.**
- **Techniques exist to maximize the benefits and minimize the drawbacks of group decision making, such as structured decision-making processes and encouraging open communication.**

## Groups Versus the Individual

- **Groups provide more complete information and diverse perspectives**, leading to broader input and increased acceptance of solutions, especially when dissent is framed as debate rather than disagreement.
- **Group decision making is time-consuming and subject to conformity pressures**, which can suppress dissent and allow dominant members to influence outcomes, potentially reducing effectiveness if their input is unreliable; ambiguous responsibility also dilutes individual accountability.
- **Groups are generally more accurate than the average individual but less accurate than the most capable member**, excel in creativity and solution acceptance, but are less efficient due to increased work hours and challenges in compiling dispersed information; effectiveness can be improved with support and guidelines that encourage open information sharing and reduce conformity.

## Groupthink and Groupshift

- **Groupthink occurs when group pressures for conformity discourage critical evaluation of alternative or minority viewpoints**, leading to poor decision-making and suppression of dissent. This is similar to the Abilene Paradox, where groups make decisions contrary to individual preferences due to conformity pressures.
- **Groupthink is intensified in larger groups, groups with strong identities, and when there is a perceived threat to the group’s positive image.** It is also more likely when groups focus on performance over learning and when members want to protect the group’s reputation.
- **In digital environments, groupthink can be amplified by online networks and social media bots**, which can rapidly spread flawed or fake ideas. Experiments show that persistent advocacy by a critical mass (about 37%) can sway group opinion, even if the idea is initially unpopular.
- **Larger groups tend to converge on popular ideas, reducing diversity of thought, while smaller groups are more likely to maintain unique perspectives.**
- **To minimize groupthink, managers should:** monitor group size, encourage impartial leadership, appoint a devil’s advocate, use exercises to stimulate discussion of diverse alternatives, and have members discuss risks before potential gains.
- **Groupshift (or group polarization) describes the tendency for group discussion to lead members to adopt more extreme versions of their initial positions.** This can result in decisions that are riskier or more cautious, depending on the group’s initial leanings.
- **Groupshift is explained by persuasive discussion, diffusion of responsibility, and the desire to differentiate from outgroups.** The direction of the shift depends on members’ pre-discussion inclinations, but it is more often toward greater risk.
- **Group decisions often exaggerate the initial positions of individual members, and understanding these dynamics is crucial for effective group decision-making.**

## Group Decision-Making Techniques

- **Interacting groups** rely on both verbal and nonverbal communication, but often face issues like self-censorship and conformity pressures (groupthink), which can limit creativity and honest input.
- **Brainstorming** encourages the free expression of all ideas without criticism to overcome conformity and stimulate creativity; however, research shows individuals working alone often generate more ideas than groups due to "production blocking," where simultaneous talking disrupts thought processes.
- **Nominal group technique** improves decision-making by having members generate ideas independently before sharing, presenting ideas one at a time without discussion, then discussing for clarity, and finally rank-ordering ideas privately; this method reduces conformity pressure and production blocking, leading to more effective idea generation.
- **Comparative strengths**: Interacting groups are effective for building commitment to solutions, brainstorming enhances group cohesiveness, and the nominal group technique is most efficient for generating a large number of ideas.
- **Selection of technique** should be based on desired outcomes and the trade-off between benefits and costs, as each method has unique advantages and limitations.
