- **Trait theories of leadership** focus on identifying personal characteristics that differentiate leaders from non-leaders, concluding that certain traits (like extraversion, conscientiousness, and openness) are consistently associated with effective leadership, but no universal set of traits guarantees leadership success.
- **Behavioral theories of leadership** emphasize what leaders do rather than their traits, identifying styles such as task-oriented and people-oriented behaviors; however, these theories are limited because they do not account for situational factors that influence leadership effectiveness.
- **Contingency theories of leadership** argue that the effectiveness of a leader’s behavior depends on the context, meaning that different situations require different leadership styles and behaviors, and leader effectiveness can change over time and across circumstances.
- The **within-person leadership perspective** highlights that leadership abilities can develop and change within individuals, emphasizing the importance of self-awareness, learning, and adaptability in leadership development.
- **Positive leadership styles** include transformational, servant, and authentic leadership, all of which foster strong, supportive relationships and encourage positive outcomes for followers and organizations.
- Leaders play a crucial role in **creating ethical organizations** by modeling ethical behavior, establishing ethical standards, and fostering a culture of integrity and accountability.
- **Building trust** is essential for leaders to have a positive impact, as trust enhances cooperation, commitment, and organizational performance.
- There are diverse **opportunities in organizational leadership**, including roles in management, consulting, training, and development, which allow individuals to influence and guide others within organizations.

## Satya Nadella’s Transformation of Microsoft

- **Satya Nadella transformed Microsoft’s culture by emphasizing empathy, psychological safety, and a shift from a “know-it-all” to a “learn-it-all” mindset**, encouraging leaders to admit gaps in knowledge and share struggles, which fostered innovation and collaboration.
- **Nadella reversed Microsoft’s adversarial stance toward open-source software**, leading to the acquisition of GitHub and signaling a move from competition to cooperation, underpinned by humility, curiosity, and adaptability.
- **Microsoft’s financial and cultural turnaround under Nadella’s leadership** is evidenced by a 1,000% increase in stock value and a market valuation of $3 trillion, with a lasting legacy of a collaborative and innovative organizational culture.
- **Effective leadership is characterized by the ability to influence others toward achieving a vision or goals**, which can arise both from formal positions and from nonsanctioned (informal) influence within organizations.
- **Leaders and managers serve distinct but complementary roles**: leaders challenge the status quo, create and inspire visions, while managers focus on planning, structuring, and overseeing daily operations for organizational effectiveness.
- **Examples like Gloria Boyland illustrate leadership through empowerment and autonomy**, showing that impactful leadership often involves stepping back to let others step up, and that not all leaders hold formal managerial roles.

## Trait Theories

- **Trait theories of leadership focus on identifying personal qualities—such as personality, social, physical, or intellectual attributes—that distinguish leaders from nonleaders.**
- The Big Five personality traits (conscientiousness, emotional stability, extraversion, openness to experience, and agreeableness) are commonly studied in relation to leadership, predicting both leadership emergence and effectiveness.
- **Possessing leadership traits does not guarantee effective or successful leadership; effectiveness also depends on situational factors.**
- Leader behavior results from an interaction between personality traits and the specific situation, aligning with situation strength theory.
- **Research using machine learning and AI shows that both individual traits and situational features are important in predicting leader effectiveness.**
- **Situational characteristics, such as power distance within a team, influence how leader traits affect outcomes; for example, extraverted leaders are more effective in teams with higher power distance because they help bridge hierarchical gaps and foster team coordination and identification.**

## Personality Traits and Leadership

- **Extraversion, conscientiousness, and openness are key personality traits linked to leadership.** Extraversion, especially its agentic (assertive) aspects, is the strongest predictor of motivation to lead, leader emergence, and transformational leadership behaviors. Conscientiousness predicts both initiating structure and consideration behaviors, and is the strongest predictor of group performance. Openness is a modest predictor of leader effectiveness and follower voice behavior.
- **The agentic aspects of extraversion (boldness, assertiveness) are more influential for leadership than affiliative aspects (warmth, sociability).** This distinction helps explain why women, stereotypically associated with affiliative traits, may be less likely to emerge as leaders.
- **Agreeableness and emotional stability contribute to follower satisfaction with leaders, but are less central to leadership effectiveness.** Openness to experience is less important overall, though it has some positive effects.
- **A proactive personality in leaders is especially valuable during organizational transitions and in teams with high relational needs.** Proactive leaders foster follower identification, engagement, and voice behavior, and are more effective when compared to less proactive predecessors.
- **Dark Triad traits (Machiavellianism, narcissism, psychopathy) are not entirely negative for leadership if present at moderate levels.** Extreme levels of these traits can harm leadership effectiveness, especially when followers have close contact with leaders and can observe toxic behaviors.
- **Trait activation theory suggests that leaders high in Dark Triad traits may act destructively when they feel unfairly treated or entitled.** This can be damaging to organizations.
- **Developing self-awareness and self-regulation can help leaders manage the negative effects of Dark Triad traits, though this is challenging, particularly for narcissists who tend to overestimate their own abilities and selectively accept feedback.**

## Emotional Intelligence and Leadership

- **Emotional intelligence (EI), especially empathy, is a key trait in effective leadership.** Empathetic leaders can sense and respond to others’ needs, listen actively, and interpret followers’ reactions.
- **Leaders who manage and express emotions appropriately can influence followers more effectively,** for example, by showing genuine sympathy or enthusiasm in relevant situations.
- **The link between leaders’ self-reported EI and transformational leadership is moderate,** but this connection is weaker when evaluated by employees rather than by the leaders themselves.
- **Individuals with high EI are more likely to emerge as leaders,** even when accounting for cognitive ability and personality traits.
- **Traits can predict who will become a leader,** but they are better at predicting leader emergence than distinguishing between effective and ineffective leaders.
- **Leadership roles and behaviors can influence and change a person’s personality,** such as increasing conscientiousness over time.
- **Trait theories are useful for predicting leadership emergence,** but they do not fully explain what makes leaders effective; behavioral theories address what successful leaders actually do.

## Behavioral Theories

- **Behavioral theories of leadership focus on the idea that leadership can be learned or trained, rather than being an inherent trait.**
- **The Ohio State Studies identified two key independent dimensions of leader behavior:** initiating structure (defining roles, organizing work, setting goals) and consideration (showing concern for followers, building trust, and respect).
- **A main limitation of behavioral theories is that they do not account for all factors influencing leadership effectiveness, such as situational variables or inherent personal traits.**

## Initiating Structure

- **Initiating structure refers to how much a leader organizes and defines roles and tasks for themselves and their followers to achieve goals.**
- This includes behaviors such as assigning tasks, setting clear performance standards, and emphasizing deadlines.
- Leaders who demonstrate initiating structure help organize work, work relationships, and goals within a group.
- **Research shows that initiating structure is strongly linked to higher group and organizational productivity, as well as more positive performance evaluations.**
- The personality trait of conscientiousness is particularly relevant for leaders who engage in initiating structure behaviors.

## Consideration

- **Consideration** refers to a leader’s efforts to build relationships based on mutual trust, respect for employees’ ideas, and concern for their feelings, including being approachable, supportive, and treating everyone equally.
- Leaders high in consideration help employees with personal issues, express appreciation, and foster a positive work environment, which leads to higher job satisfaction, motivation, lower stress, and greater respect from followers.
- Both **initiating structure** and consideration are linked to leadership development and succession planning; individuals who display these behaviors are more likely to be selected for leadership roles.
- Leaders’ emotions and traits, such as pride and self-confidence (when not excessive), influence their likelihood of engaging in consideration and initiating structure behaviors.
- The situation affects leadership behavior: during widespread understaffing, leaders focus more on initiating structure to maintain efficiency, while during expertise understaffing, they emphasize consideration to retain key talent.
- Organizational context and individual managers have a greater impact on the use of consideration and initiating structure than national culture.

## An Ethical Choice

- **Nudging is a leadership technique that subtly alters decision-making environments to guide people toward desirable outcomes, often by changing default choices.**
- A common example is automatic enrollment in organ donation programs, which significantly increases participation rates (from less than 30% to 80%) while still allowing individuals to opt out.
- **Nudging can promote ethical behavior**, such as requiring insurance claimants to record a video, which increases honesty compared to written claims.
- **Traditional performance evaluations often overlook the processes leaders use, focusing mainly on financial outcomes**, which can allow abusive leadership to go undetected. Nudging can address this by shifting to process-oriented performance management, emphasizing specific actions and continuous feedback.
- **Challenges of nudging include perceptions of manipulation or patronization.** To address these, managers should:
    - Be transparent about the purpose of nudges, as disclosure does not reduce their effectiveness and can build trust.
    - Involve employees in co-creating nudges to respect autonomy and tailor solutions to real needs, increasing effectiveness.
    - Use constructive framing, presenting nudges as supportive feedback rather than as error prevention, to enhance motivation and acceptance.

## Comparing Trait Theories and Behavioral Theories

- **Both trait and behavioral theories contribute to understanding leadership**, as each explains different aspects of how leaders emerge and how effective they are.
- **Determining the relationship between traits, behaviors, and leadership outcomes is complex**, involving challenges such as identifying which traits or behaviors predict outcomes, understanding which combinations are effective, and establishing causality.
- **Context plays a significant role in leadership effectiveness**, meaning that even leaders with ideal traits and behaviors may not succeed if the situation is not favorable, leading to the development of contingency theories.

## Contingency Theories

- **Contingency theories of leadership emphasize that effective leadership depends on matching leader behavior to the specific needs of the situation, rather than relying on a fixed style.**
- Leaders can vary their approach over time, such as starting with crisis management actions (cost-cutting, restructuring, direct communication) and later shifting to strategies for long-term growth and innovation as circumstances change.
- The effectiveness of a leader is often determined by their ability to diagnose problems, adapt their behavior, and align their actions with the evolving demands of the organization and environment.

## The Fiedler Model

- **Fiedler's contingency model states that group performance depends on matching a leader’s style with the degree of situational control.**
- **Leadership styles are categorized as task-oriented or relationship-oriented, and these styles are considered stable traits.**
- **Three key situational dimensions determine the amount of control a leader has:**
    - Leader–member relations: the level of trust, confidence, and respect followers have for the leader.
    - Task structure: how clearly jobs and assignments are defined (structured vs. unstructured).
    - Position power: the leader’s authority over hiring, firing, promotions, and rewards.
- **A leader has more control when leader–member relations are good, tasks are highly structured, and position power is strong.**
- **Task-oriented leaders perform best in situations that are either very favorable or very unfavorable (extremes of control).**
- **Relationship-oriented leaders perform best in moderately favorable situations (moderate control).**
- **Some research has challenged the model, showing that relationship-oriented leaders can also perform well in favorable conditions, and the model is less frequently studied today.**
- **The model contributed to understanding the impact of situational factors on leadership effectiveness, but newer theories have expanded on these ideas.**

## Situational Leadership Theory

- **Situational Leadership Theory (SLT) emphasizes that effective leadership depends on matching leadership style to followers’ readiness**, which is defined by their commitment and competence for a specific task.
- **SLT identifies four leadership styles based on combinations of directive and supportive behaviors**: telling/directing, selling/coaching, participating/supporting, and delegating.
- **Telling/directing is used when followers lack competence but are committed**, requiring clear, specific instructions from the leader.
- **Selling/coaching is appropriate when followers have some competence but low commitment**, so leaders must provide both direction and motivational support.
- **Participating/supporting is for followers who are competent but variably committed**, where leaders reduce direction but maintain support and encouragement.
- **Delegating is suitable when followers are both competent and committed**, allowing leaders to hand over responsibility and minimize involvement.
- **SLT’s main contribution is its focus on adapting leadership to follower readiness**, but research has found issues with the model’s internal consistency and empirical support, so its practical application should be approached cautiously.

## Follower Contingency Theories

- **Leadership is a dynamic relationship between leaders and followers**, not a one-way process; followers play a crucial role in shaping leader effectiveness.
- **Leader–Participation Model** connects leadership behavior to the degree of subordinate participation in decision making, emphasizing that leader behavior should adapt to the task structure and situation. It identifies when involving subordinates in decisions is most effective for gaining their acceptance and commitment.
- **Shared Leadership Theory** proposes that leadership can be distributed among team members, with multiple individuals influencing each other and taking on leadership roles as needed. This approach is most effective in teams with a shared purpose, flexible roles, psychological safety, and autonomy, and is linked to improved team performance, creativity, and innovation, especially in complex tasks.
- Shared leadership is particularly useful in volatile or high-pressure environments (e.g., emergency medical teams), but can also lead to conflict if power dynamics among members are unbalanced.
- **Followership Theory** shifts focus to the impact of followers on leadership success, highlighting that effective followers are industrious, enthusiastic, reliable, and proactive in supporting the leader’s vision. Poor followership (e.g., insubordination, conformity, lack of initiative) can undermine group goals and leader effectiveness.
- Perceptions of ideal follower traits (such as being a “good citizen” or team player) influence both follower and leader performance evaluations. Authenticity and proactive relationship-building with leaders (“managing up”) enhance follower effectiveness and contribute to organizational success.
- Both followership and “managing up” involve understanding and adapting to leaders’ goals and styles, aiming to build productive, mutually beneficial working relationships.

## Dynamic Leadership: A Within-Person Perspective

- **Traditional leadership theories assume leadership is a stable quality**, focusing on traits, behaviors, or styles that are consistent over time and across situations.
- **These models seek to identify enduring characteristics or patterns** that distinguish effective leaders, suggesting predictability in leadership behavior.
- **While this approach has provided important insights**, it may miss the reality that leadership can be more dynamic and variable within individuals, depending on context and circumstances.

## The Within-Person Leadership Perspective

- **Leadership is viewed as a dynamic, context-sensitive process** that can change not only over long periods but also from day to day or hour to hour for the same individual.
- **Within-person leadership research focuses on how a single leader’s behaviors and attitudes shift in response to evolving demands and circumstances**, rather than comparing different leaders or assuming stable traits.
- **Research methods like daily diaries and experience sampling reveal that leaders adapt their behavior**—for example, being optimistic at the start of the week, becoming directive under pressure, or appearing withdrawn after stress.
- **Moment-to-moment changes in leadership are seen as adaptive responses, not inconsistency**, highlighting the responsive nature of effective leadership.
- **This approach emphasizes the impact of specific events (such as conflict, fatigue, or team dynamics) on a leader’s thoughts, feelings, and actions in real time**, providing a more precise understanding of how leadership unfolds in context.

## Key Insights From Within-Person Research

- **Leadership behaviors are not fixed traits; they fluctuate within the same individual over short periods.** For example, a leader can display transformational, transactional, and controlling behaviors all in one day.
- **Within-person studies reveal that daily factors, such as sleep quality and mood, significantly impact leadership behavior.** Poor sleep can reduce self-control and increase abusive actions, while positive energy boosts transformational leadership that motivates employees.
- **Daily fluctuations in leadership behavior affect how followers experience leadership and underscore the importance of supporting leaders in real time.**
- **The within-person perspective shifts the focus from fixed leadership styles to adaptability, emotional regulation, and situational responsiveness.** Leaders who can recognize and adjust their behaviors are more effective across different contexts.
- **Organizations should prioritize leadership development that fosters real-time reflection, understanding of behavioral drivers, and adaptive decision-making, rather than just consistency.**

## Positive Leadership Styles and Relationships

- **Leader–member exchange (LMX) theory emphasizes that leaders develop unique, individualized relationships with each follower**, resulting in varying levels of trust, support, and communication within a team.
- **Charismatic leadership theory focuses on leaders who inspire and energize followers through their vision, personal charm, and emotional appeal**, capturing attention and motivating people to achieve shared goals.
- **The full range leadership model describes a spectrum of positive leadership behaviors**, highlighting how leaders can engage followers through transformational and transactional approaches to maximize motivation and performance.

## Leader–Member Exchange (LMX) Theory

- **Leader–Member Exchange (LMX) theory explains that leaders form unique, individualized relationships with each follower, resulting in varying relationship quality.**
- **A select group of followers, known as the ingroup, receive more trust, attention, and special privileges from the leader, while others form the outgroup.**
- **High-quality LMX relationships are more likely with followers who are competent, proactive, positive, and sometimes ingratiating or self-promoting.**
- **Leaders foster higher LMX by showing emotional labor, transformational leadership, rewarding desired behaviors, and punishing undesired ones.**
- **Social capital is built as leaders and followers reciprocate contributions, often increasing when they feel obligated and decreasing when they feel their contribution is sufficient.**
- **High-quality LMX relationships develop over time, especially when leaders and followers share similarities, mutual liking, trust, and high expectations; same-gender pairs often have closer relationships.**
- **Not all employees value LMX equally; its importance varies among individuals.**
- **Research supports that leaders differentiate among followers, with ingroup members receiving higher performance ratings, engaging in more organizational citizenship behaviors (OCBs), fewer counterproductive work behaviors (CWBs), and reporting greater satisfaction.**
- **LMX enhances trust, motivation, empowerment, job satisfaction, psychological and physical well-being, and reduces stress and burnout.**
- **Strong LMX is linked to better career outcomes, including higher salaries, bonuses, and promotions.**

## Charismatic Leadership

- **Charismatic leadership is characterized by leaders who inspire followers through vision, values-driven behavior, and emotional appeal.** These leaders are seen as possessing extraordinary qualities, often attributed to their ability to unite and transform people.
- **Key traits of charismatic leaders include having a compelling vision, willingness to take personal risks, sensitivity to follower needs, and engaging in unconventional behaviors.** Traits such as intelligence, extraversion, agreeableness, and emotional intelligence help charismatic leaders motivate and influence others.
- **Charismatic leaders influence followers by articulating an appealing vision, using passionate communication, and setting high performance expectations.** They build followers’ self-esteem, foster cooperation, and serve as role models through their words and actions.
- **The effectiveness of charismatic leadership is enhanced in situations of stress or crisis, and when ambiguity is high.** Followers are more receptive to charismatic leaders during uncertain times, and ambiguous situations make people rely more on charismatic cues to judge leadership effectiveness.
- **Charismatic leadership can have a “dark side,” as it may be associated with narcissism and can distort followers’ perceptions of fairness.** Charismatic leaders may misuse their influence for personal gain, as seen in cases of corporate fraud and unethical behavior.
- **The success of charismatic leadership depends on the situation, the leader’s vision, and the presence of organizational checks and balances.** While charismatic leadership can be highly effective, it is not always the best solution and must be monitored to prevent misuse.

## The Full Range Leadership Model

- **The full range leadership model identifies a continuum of leadership styles from passive and ineffective (laissez-faire) to active and effective (transformational).**
- **Laissez-faire leadership is the most passive and least effective style, characterized by leaders who avoid decision-making and responsibility.** This approach leads to poor team performance, low-quality leader-member exchange (LMX), decreased organizational commitment, increased role conflict, ambiguity, and a stressful work environment, especially for employees experiencing mistreatment.
- **Transactional leadership focuses on guiding followers toward established goals through clarifying roles, rewarding or punishing performance, and intervening when necessary.** Management by exception (intervening only when problems arise) is generally ineffective, especially in its passive form. Contingent reward leadership (providing rewards for meeting expectations) can be effective but does not inspire extra effort or commitment.
- **Transformational leadership inspires followers to transcend self-interest for the good of the organization, leading to extraordinary effects on followers and organizational outcomes.** This style is more prevalent in certain industries (like health and security) and cultures that value assertiveness and egalitarianism.
- **Transformational leadership is characterized by the “four I’s”: individualized consideration, intellectual stimulation, inspirational motivation, and idealized influence.** These qualities result in higher productivity, creativity, morale, satisfaction, organizational effectiveness, psychological safety, well-being, lower turnover, absenteeism, and greater adaptability.

## Virtual Leadership

- **Virtual leadership involves influencing teams primarily through technology when members are geographically or temporally dispersed**, requiring leaders to adapt their approach beyond simply replicating in-person behaviors online.
- **Unique challenges in virtual leadership include limited informal interactions, reduced nonverbal cues, and increased risks of social fragmentation**, which can lead to isolation, blurred boundaries, and uneven participation among team members.
- **Leaders must proactively increase clarity, visibility, and connection** to overcome these barriers, using strategies such as articulating a clear mission, setting explicit goals and norms, fostering a supportive climate, enabling effective use of technology, and encouraging self-management.
- **Empowering and shared-leadership behaviors have a stronger positive impact on performance in virtual teams** compared to co-located teams, and high-quality leader-member exchange (LMX) is crucial for reducing isolation and supporting innovation.
- **Effective virtual leaders translate traditional leadership behaviors into digital actions**, such as posting visible progress milestones, modeling respectful communication norms, and starting meetings with personal check-ins.
- **The effectiveness of virtual leadership is especially important for tasks that are interdependent and complex**, where alignment and predictable communication are critical.
- **In hybrid teams, leaders must address proximity bias by ensuring equal access to information and influence for both remote and in-person members**.
- **Multimodal leadership—choosing the right communication medium for the task and team—is an essential skill for virtual leaders**.
- **Virtual leadership represents an intentional evolution of classic leadership principles, adapted for digitally connected and geographically distributed teams**.

## Integrating and Evaluating Positive Leadership Styles

- **Laissez-faire leadership is generally ineffective**, often resulting in negative outcomes like social loafing, poor relationships, and toxic environments.
- **Transformational leadership consistently produces the best outcomes** compared to charismatic and transactional styles, especially in group performance, organizational citizenship behaviors (OCBs), and satisfaction with the leader.
- **Charismatic leadership focuses on how leaders communicate** (passion, dynamism), while **transformational leadership emphasizes what is communicated** (individualization, intellectual stimulation, inspiration, influence). Both inspire followers, but transformational leadership is broader in scope.
- **Transactional and transformational leadership are complementary**; transformational leadership builds on transactional methods, leading to higher effort and performance than transactional leadership alone.
- **Transactional leadership’s contingent reward is effective**, but management by exception (intervening only when problems arise) has mixed effects: it can empower employees but may harm leader–member exchange (LMX) relationships and reduce relationship quality.
- **Situational awareness is crucial**; the effectiveness of a leadership style depends on the context and potential unintended side effects.
- **Transformational leadership is effective due to five mechanisms**:
    - Affective/attitudinal: Improves moods, job satisfaction, commitment, and well-being.
    - Motivational: Increases confidence, engagement, and willingness to exert effort.
    - Identification: Fosters personal identification with the leader and organization.
    - Social exchange: Enhances leader–follower relationship quality (LMX), which is the primary driver of positive outcomes.
    - Justice enhancement: Improves perceptions of fairness and trust.
- **Social exchange (LMX) is the main pathway** through which transformational leadership influences outcomes; other mechanisms often work by first improving this relationship.
- **Potential downsides of transformational leadership** include the risk of manipulation, emotional exhaustion for leaders, and the possibility of charismatic leaders with unethical motives (e.g., narcissists) undermining team functioning.
- **Critiques of transformational leadership theory** involve unclear dimensions, measurement issues (actual behaviors vs. follower perceptions), and the risk of conflating leadership with desirable outcomes.
- **Assessing leaders requires attention to their values, integrity, and the sustainability of their influence**, not just their charisma or surface appeal.

## The Ethical (and Unethical) Aspects of Leadership

- **Leaders play a crucial role in establishing and maintaining ethical standards within organizations**, shaping the ethical climate and influencing the behavior of others.
- **Ethics and trust are fundamental components of effective leadership**, going beyond traditional leadership theories by directly addressing how leaders can foster ethical conduct.
- **Contemporary leadership concepts focus on how leaders can actively create ethical organizations**, emphasizing the importance of leaders modeling ethical behavior and building trust among followers.

## Authentic Leadership

- **Authentic leadership involves self-awareness, adherence to personal mission and principles, consideration of others' perspectives, and genuine interactions with employees.**
- **Leaders who are authentic foster trust and faith among their followers, as seen in examples like Sundar Pichai's transparent leadership at Google.**
- **Authentic leadership is strongly linked to positive outcomes such as improved group performance, organizational citizenship behaviors (OCBs), leader-member exchange (LMX), satisfaction, trust in the leader, perceived leader effectiveness, follower attitudes, and empowerment.**
- **There are also moderate links between authentic leadership and follower creativity, engagement, reduced deviance, lower turnover intentions, and decreased burnout.**
- **Followers are more likely to trust and commit to leaders who consistently act according to their stated values and principles.**
- **A double standard exists for women: women are generally seen as more authentic when displaying traditionally feminine or warm leadership styles, but may be perceived as inauthentic if they use assertive or directive behaviors.**
- **Transformational and authentic leadership share many similarities, with some researchers viewing them as nearly identical, but authentic leadership may be especially important for team performance and motivating OCBs due to its focus on internalized mission.**

## Toward a Better World

- **CSR initiatives require knowledgeable, skilled, and influential leadership**; they do not occur spontaneously but depend on collective effort and team learning to develop necessary skills, make a business case, maximize impact, and measure success.
- **CSR managers often face challenges influencing other managers**, especially when CSR tasks are perceived as misaligned with financial goals, highlighting the need for effective training and development.
- **Training programs like iCSR provide practical development for CSR leaders**, offering virtual cohorts, networking, and instruction from experienced executives from major corporations.
- **The iCSR curriculum emphasizes authentic, ethical, and servant leadership**, teaching participants about the history and context of CSR, building internal buy-in, ethical decision-making, stakeholder engagement, and effective communication of CSR impact.
- **Trainees develop both leadership behaviors and practical skills**, such as creating internal policies, fostering employee volunteerism, and building external partnerships for philanthropic investment.
- **Research indicates that leaders’ skills and experiences significantly influence CSR strategy and effectiveness**, but more systematic research is needed to fully assess the impact of CSR leadership training programs.
- **Effective CSR training programs foster a strong learning culture, encourage peer interaction, and focus on mastery of CSR skills rather than self-validation or fear of failure.**

## Ethical Leadership

- **Ethical leadership involves leaders acting as ethical role models**, demonstrating appropriate behavior, using power ethically, and treating others fairly.
- **It directly shapes employees' moral thinking and organizational culture**, establishing clear values and principles for ethical behavior.
- **Ethical leadership sets standards for interpersonal treatment**, reducing counterproductive work behaviors (CWBs) such as intimidation and excuses, empowering employees to address injustice, and improving customer service by clarifying moral reasons for positive behavior.
- **It enhances followers' job attitudes and perceptions of leaders**, increasing trust in leadership, which leads to positive organizational outcomes.
- **Ethical leadership positively impacts performance**, boosting task performance and organizational citizenship behavior (OCB) by improving leader-member relationships (LMX), fostering an ethical culture, and strengthening organizational identification.

## Unethical Leadership

- **Ethical leadership is often difficult to distinguish from transformational leadership and can be inconsistent due to factors like stress, mental exhaustion, and a sense of entitlement from past ethical behavior.**
- **Unethical leadership involves leaders serving as negative role models by abusing power or treating others unfairly; followers may still support unethical leaders if their values align and mutual trust exists.**
- **The effectiveness of ethical leadership decreases when there are too many followers, when followers have differing moral perspectives, or when followers lack mindfulness; ethical leadership impacts not only behavior but also financial outcomes, such as reducing retail shrink through decreased incivility.**

## Servant Leadership

- **Servant leadership prioritizes the growth and development of followers over self-interest**, emphasizing behaviors such as listening, empathy, persuasion, acceptance, stewardship, and actively fostering followers’ potential.
- **Servant leadership is closely linked to positive follower outcomes**, including improved job attitudes, increased trust in leadership, and higher leader-member exchange (LMX) perceptions.
- **This leadership style predicts enhanced follower job performance and organizational citizenship behavior (OCB)**, though these effects are somewhat less pronounced than its impact on well-being and leadership quality, possibly due to followers’ feelings of gratitude and reciprocity.
- **Women tend to benefit more from servant leadership**, as the style aligns with traditional gender role expectations, leading to higher follower performance compared to male servant leaders.
- **While servant leadership shares similarities with transformational, authentic, and ethical leadership**, it is considered the most distinct and potentially unique among these leadership styles.

## Abusive Supervision

- **Abusive supervision involves hostile behaviors from managers, both verbal and nonverbal, such as outbursts, public ridicule, and inappropriate blaming, and is perceived as a form of unethical leadership.**
- **A sense of injustice, especially lack of interpersonal justice, is strongly linked to abusive supervision; personality traits like negative affect, narcissism, and poor self-control in supervisors increase the likelihood of abusive behaviors.**
- **Supervisors may use abusive supervision as a coping mechanism for their own stress, overload, and emotional exhaustion, which can paradoxically increase their work engagement.**
- **Followers’ characteristics, such as having a negative worldview, can make them more likely to report experiencing abusive supervision; both poor and high performers can be targeted, for reasons ranging from perceived incompetence to jealousy or threat.**
- **Abusive supervision has severe consequences for employees, including increased depression, emotional exhaustion, lower self-esteem, dehumanization, ostracism, job tension, decreased organizational commitment and job satisfaction, and increased work–family conflict.**
- **Victims of abusive supervision are more likely to engage in counterproductive work behaviors (CWBs) and less likely to engage in organizational citizenship behaviors (OCBs); coworkers who witness abuse may work harder out of fear or, in ethical climates, may support victims and challenge abusers.**
- **Abusive supervision can create a vicious cycle, where victims retaliate with deviant behaviors, prompting further abuse from supervisors.**
- **Abusive behaviors can be learned and perpetuated by new leaders who view abusive supervisors as role models or who see such behaviors as effective leadership; discussing abusive supervision can also normalize it.**
- **Leaders with strong moral identities who distance themselves from abusive role models are less likely to become abusive and may lead more ethically.**
- **Employees with conscientiousness, resilience, and self-regulation cope better with abusive supervision.**
- **Followership theory suggests that followers can sometimes confront and manage abusive supervisors by demonstrating their value, but the primary responsibility for stopping abuse lies with leaders.**
- **Leaders can be trained to support subordinates and avoid abusive behaviors; feelings of guilt or shame after abusive episodes can lead to more constructive leadership, provided these responses are genuine.**

## Leadership and Trust

- **Trust is a psychological state involving mutual positive expectations and concern for each other's welfare, essential for effective leadership.**
- **Trust can be based on competence (skills and experience) or integrity (motives, honesty, character), with integrity-based trust being more effective for building new relationships, especially in business contexts.**
- **Leaders who foster trust positively impact group performance, as followers are confident their rights and interests are protected, and leaders are assured followers will fulfill their duties; transformational leaders build support by demonstrating their actions benefit everyone.**
- **Felt trust enhances effectiveness—leaders who feel trusted are more effective, and followers who feel trusted become more engaged and autonomous, increasing positive leader-member exchange (LMX) perceptions.**

## The Outcomes of Trust

- **Trust encourages risk-taking**: When you trust your supervisor or leader, you are more willing to try new approaches or follow new directions, even if it means deviating from established routines.
- **Trust facilitates information sharing**: You are more likely to share your concerns and ideas at work when you feel psychologically safe, knowing your manager will listen and consider your input.
- **Trusting groups are more effective**: In groups where trust is present, members help each other more, communicate openly, and put in extra effort, which strengthens group performance. In contrast, mistrust leads to suspicion, guarded behavior, and poor communication, which can harm the group.
- **Trust enhances productivity**: Employees who trust their supervisors tend to be more productive and receive higher performance ratings. Mistrust, on the other hand, leads to withholding information and self-serving behavior, which reduces overall effectiveness.

## Trust Development

- **Trust in leaders is based on three main characteristics: integrity, benevolence, and ability.**
- **Integrity** involves honesty, truthfulness, and consistency between words and actions (authenticity); it is rated as the most important leadership attribute by employees.
- **Benevolence** means the leader genuinely cares about your interests, demonstrating supportive and caring behavior that strengthens the emotional bond with followers.
- **Ability** refers to the leader’s technical and interpersonal skills; trust is unlikely if you doubt a leader’s competence, regardless of their integrity or intentions.

## Trust Propensity

- **Trust propensity** is the likelihood that an employee will trust a leader, and it varies among individuals.
- Trust propensity is strongly associated with the personality trait of **agreeableness**; individuals with higher agreeableness are more likely to trust others.
- People with **lower self-esteem** tend to have a lower propensity to trust others.
- Teams with members who have varying levels of trust propensity are more vulnerable to negative outcomes, especially when conflicts occur.
- **Certainty about a leader's trustworthiness** increases employees' propensity to trust, particularly when the leader has significant consequences for being untrustworthy.

## Trust and Culture

- **Trust in the workplace is shaped by cultural context**, with different cultures valuing different leadership styles and behaviors.
- In collectivistic cultures like Taiwan, **paternalistic leadership that is benevolent and ethical fosters employee trust**, aligning with Confucian values that emphasize hierarchy and relationships.
- In individualistic cultures, **employees may resist paternalistic leadership** and instead develop trust based on leadership support and consistency, reflecting a preference for individual autonomy.

## The Role of Time in Trust

- **Trust is developed over time by observing consistent, positive behavior**, such as fairness and competence from leaders, especially in new or changing organizational contexts.
- **Leaders build trust by demonstrating integrity, benevolence, and ability**, particularly in situations where they could act opportunistically or disappoint employees.
- **Admitting and apologizing for mistakes helps maintain trust**, while denying errors can damage it.
- **Shifting from top-down communication to ongoing, inclusive dialogue fosters trust**, as regular, interactive, and intimate conversations between leaders and employees increase engagement and trust.
- **Trust can be breached when leaders switch from transformational to laissez-faire leadership or act unethically**, leading followers to perceive them as less effective.

## Regaining Trust

- **Breaking the psychological contract with employees leads to negative outcomes** such as lower satisfaction, reduced commitment, higher turnover intentions, decreased organizational citizenship behavior (OCB), and poorer task performance.
- **Regaining trust depends on the type of violation:** If the issue is a lack of ability, acknowledging the mistake and apologizing is most effective; if the issue is a lack of integrity, apologies are generally ineffective.
- **Ignoring the violation or refusing to address it never helps restore trust.**
- **Trust can be rebuilt through consistent trustworthy behavior,** but timely apologies are more effective than delayed ones.
- **If deception was involved, trust is never fully restored,** regardless of apologies or subsequent trustworthy actions.

## Opportunities in Organizational Leadership

- **Leaders play a significant role in organizations, but their influence is limited by factors beyond their control.**
- **Leadership is complex and involves many unpredictable variables, making it a challenging and sometimes "messy" practice.**
- **Opportunities exist for managers to use knowledge from organizational behavior (OB) to identify, develop, and maximize leadership potential within organizations.**

## Identifying and Selecting Leaders

- **Succession planning is critical for organizations** because leadership changes are inevitable, and poor planning can lead to ineffective appointments and organizational decline, as illustrated by JCPenney’s leadership struggles and eventual bankruptcy.
- **Selecting effective leaders involves identifying the necessary knowledge, skills, and abilities for the role**, which differ by management level: executive leaders require conceptual, problem-solving, social, and strategic skills; middle managers need interpersonal, social judgment, and people skills; supervisory leaders benefit from these plus technical skills and knowledge.
- **Personality traits such as extraversion, conscientiousness, and openness to experience are linked to leadership**, but relying solely on a candidate’s broad experience or track record is less effective than considering situation-specific experience relevant to the organization’s needs.

## Training and Developing Leaders

- **Effective leadership training starts with a thorough needs analysis**, where you assess current staffing requirements and evaluate the existing leadership talent pool to identify specific training needs.
- **Clearly define the leadership skills to be developed**, such as interpersonal or cross-cultural communication skills, especially for roles involving diverse interactions.
- **Evaluate the transfer of training to the workplace** by checking if leaders apply what they learned, considering both opportunities and motivation to use new skills.
- **Regular review of leadership performance after key events** helps determine if leaders have developed necessary skills and informs decisions about promotion, retention, demotion, or lateral transfers.
- **Executive coaching offers significant benefits**, with studies showing a 788% return on investment due to increased productivity and employee retention, functioning as a personalized development approach similar to mentorship.

## Myth or Science?

- **Leadership behaviors, skills, and styles are key predictors of employee productivity, satisfaction, team performance, and overall organizational success.**
- **Research shows that leadership skills can be developed through training programs, leading to improved workplace performance.**
- Not all leadership training is equally effective; only about 10% of corporate leadership training spending results in tangible outcomes.
- Programs based on a needs analysis—identifying specific leadership skills required—produce greater learning and skill transfer.
- Mandatory leadership training programs are more effective at achieving organizational goals than voluntary ones.
- Short, intensive training programs yield the most significant improvements in leadership skills.
- Training that includes opportunities for employees to practice new skills is more effective than programs that only provide information.

## Leaders as Mentors

- **Mentoring involves a senior employee (mentor) supporting a less-experienced employee (protégé), serving both career advancement and psychosocial support functions.**
- **A successful mentor-protégé relationship is built on mutual trust, accountability, confidence, adaptability, and aims to enhance the protégé’s competence and career progression, often through networking.**
- **Formal mentoring matches protégés with mentors based on leadership potential and organizational function, while informal mentoring arises naturally when a leader identifies potential in a less-experienced employee and tests them with challenging assignments.**
- **Both formal and informal mentoring aim to teach protégés how the organization operates beyond formal structures and procedures.**
- **Not all employees participate in mentoring, but research shows that mentoring programs benefit both mentors and protégés, with informal mentoring arrangements generally being more effective.**
