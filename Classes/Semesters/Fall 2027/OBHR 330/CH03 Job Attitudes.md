## Chapter 3

- **Attitudes consist of three components:** cognitive (beliefs or thoughts), affective (feelings or emotions), and behavioral (intentions to act).
- **Attitudes and behavior are related:** your attitudes can influence your behavior, but the strength of this relationship depends on factors like the importance of the attitude and the presence of social pressures.
- **Major job attitudes include:** job satisfaction, job involvement, organizational commitment, and perceived organizational support.
- **Job satisfaction can be measured using two main approaches:** the single global rating method (overall satisfaction) and the summation score method (satisfaction with specific job facets).
- **Main causes of job satisfaction:** the nature of the work itself, pay, advancement opportunities, supervision, and coworker relationships.
- **Key outcomes of job satisfaction:** higher job performance, increased organizational citizenship behavior, greater customer satisfaction, and reduced absenteeism and turnover.
- **Four responses to job dissatisfaction:** exit (leaving the organization), voice (attempting to improve conditions), loyalty (waiting for improvement), and neglect (reducing work effort).

## The Era of Gamification

- **Employee engagement is at an 11-year low**, with workers reporting less job satisfaction and weaker connections to their organization’s mission or purpose.
- **Gamification in the workplace involves applying game elements**—such as scoring, competition, and leaderboards—to work activities, making routine tasks more engaging and achievements more visible and celebrated.
- **Strategic gamification can increase engagement, productivity, retention, willingness to try new things, risk-taking, and motivation for repetitive tasks.**
- **Ansay & Associates successfully uses gamification** by recognizing high-performing employees through the “Wheel of Wow,” where selected employees spin for prizes, leading to increased engagement and industry recognition.
- **Research supports the effectiveness of gamification:** A study of 1,431 U.S. workers found that a lottery-based rewards program outperformed traditional rewards in boosting motivation and performance.
- **Gamification enhances intrinsic motivation** by allowing employees to make decisions, experience progress, and collaborate with others who share similar interests.
- **Gamification is not universally effective:** For example, United Airlines discontinued a lottery program when employees perceived it as a cost-saving measure rather than a genuine reward.
- **Job attitudes, such as engagement, are key drivers of behavior** in the workplace, influenced by factors beyond just culture, leadership, and infrastructure.

## Attitudes

- **Attitudes consist of three key components: cognition, affect, and behavior.** Cognition involves your beliefs or opinions about something (e.g., "My pay is low"), affect refers to your emotional response (e.g., "I am angry over how little I’m paid"), and behavior is your intention to act in a certain way (e.g., "I’m going to look for another job that pays better").
- **These components are interconnected and influence each other.** For example, your belief that you were treated unfairly (cognition) can lead to feelings of anger or hurt (affect), which may result in actions like avoiding or confronting the person involved (behavior).
- **The three-component model (the ABCs of attitudes) helps explain the complexity of attitudes and their impact on workplace behavior.** For instance, if you think you deserved a promotion (cognition), dislike your supervisor as a result (affect), and complain or take action (behavior), this sequence shows how attitudes shape your actions at work.

## Attitudes and Behavior

- **Attitudes generally predict future behavior**, but the strength of this relationship depends on several factors: the importance of the attitude, how closely the attitude matches the behavior, how accessible the attitude is, the presence of social pressures, and whether the attitude is based on direct experience.
- **Important attitudes**—those tied to your core values, self-interest, or identification with valued groups—are more likely to influence your behavior than peripheral attitudes. For example, disliking your supervisor (an important attitude) is more likely to affect your actions than disliking your office chair (a peripheral attitude).
- **Attitude-behavior correspondence** means that attitudes are more likely to affect behaviors that directly relate to them. For instance, your attitude toward your supervisor will more strongly influence your behavior toward them than toward unrelated coworkers.
- **Attitude accessibility** refers to how easily an attitude comes to mind; attitudes you frequently express or think about are more likely to guide your behavior.
- **Direct experience with an attitude object** strengthens the attitude-behavior link. You are more likely to act on attitudes formed from personal experience than those based on indirect information.
- **Behavior can also influence attitudes**, especially when people adjust their attitudes to align with their actions to avoid contradictions, a process explained by cognitive dissonance theory.
- **Cognitive dissonance** occurs when there is inconsistency between attitudes and behaviors, creating discomfort that motivates you to change your attitude, behavior, or rationalize the discrepancy. For example, justifying texting while driving despite knowing it's unsafe is a way to reduce dissonance.
- **The motivation to reduce cognitive dissonance** depends on the importance of the conflicting elements, the degree of control you believe you have, and the presence of rewards. Dissonance is less distressing if accompanied by positive outcomes, such as a reward or improved mood.

## Job Attitudes

- **Organizational behavior focuses on a select group of job attitudes** that reflect how employees evaluate their work and organization, with organizational identification being a key example.
- **Organizational identification** refers to how much you define yourself by the same characteristics as your organization, which strongly influences your attitudes and behaviors at work.
- **Attitudes are shaped by ongoing experiences**—for example, repeated negative events like pay cuts or canceled bonuses can lead you to form negative attitudes and disidentify with your organization.
- **Other major job attitudes include job satisfaction, organizational commitment, perceived organizational support (POS), and employee engagement**, all of which play important roles in how you feel and act at work.
- **Both positive and negative job attitudes are important**, as negative attitudes can have significant outcomes for individuals and organizations.
- **Organizational identification can have negative effects**, such as encouraging unethical behavior to benefit the organization, reducing performance, and increasing workplace conflict.
- **Changes in organizational leadership can impact identification**; for instance, white male executives showed decreased organizational identification and were less helpful to colleagues after a person of color was appointed CEO.
- **Strong organizational identification may reduce intervention in negative situations**, such as being less likely to address incivility toward coworkers.

## Job Satisfaction

- **Job satisfaction refers to a positive feeling about a job that results from evaluating its characteristics.**
- **High job satisfaction means you have positive feelings about your work, while low job satisfaction means you have negative feelings.**
- **Job satisfaction is considered one of the most important employee attitudes.**

## Ethical Choice

- **Office talk can have both positive and negative effects**—it can help employees process information and solve problems, but can also be damaging, such as when it involves "stress bragging," which reduces willingness to help and increases burnout among coworkers.
- **Venting in the workplace can be beneficial** for high-performing, engaged employees, as it helps restore a positive attitude, builds camaraderie, and fosters empathy, but can also lead to increased venting or withdrawal if concerns are not heard.
- **There are no clear-cut rules for acceptable office talk**, so you need to monitor your own conversations, be aware of situational norms, your role, the discussion content, and your engagement, and decide when to participate or excuse yourself.
- **Psychological empowerment**—the belief in your impact, competence, job meaningfulness, and autonomy—strongly predicts positive job attitudes and reduces strain, and moderately predicts performance and organizational citizenship behaviors.
- **Empowerment is most effective when all four beliefs (impact, competency, meaningfulness, autonomy) are considered together**, with meaningfulness having a particularly strong effect on attitudes and strain, even when other factors are accounted for.

## Organizational Commitment

- **Organizational commitment** involves an employee's identification with their organization, alignment with its goals, and desire to remain a member, with emotional attachment and value alignment being central.
- **Three types of commitment** are identified: affective (emotional attachment), continuance (perceived cost of leaving), and normative (sense of obligation). Affective commitment is the strongest predictor of behaviors like turnover, retention, attendance, performance, and organizational citizenship.
- **Continuance commitment** becomes more relevant when employees have alternative job options, while **normative commitment** is significant when personal values are challenged.
- Commitment can be directed toward various targets, such as the organization, team, supervisor, or coworkers, and not just the organization as a whole.
- Employees typically exhibit low, moderate, or high levels across all three types of commitment, but some may show high levels in only one type, especially affective or normative.
- **Patterns of commitment**—the combination and levels of the three types—significantly impact organizational outcomes.

## Perceived Organizational Support

- **Perceived organizational support (POS)** refers to how much employees believe their organization values their contributions and cares about their well-being.
- **Supportive organizations** are characterized by fair treatment, high-quality relationships, and practices that are seen as supportive, developmental, and fair.
- **POS can be motivated by various reasons**, including genuine care for employees, a desire to retain talent, or as a strategic investment; organizations often have mixed motives.
- **Cultural context influences POS:** In low-power-distance cultures (like the U.S.), employees expect and seek organizational support, viewing work as an exchange rather than a duty, and POS has been increasing over time in these settings.
- In **high-power-distance cultures** (like China), POS is less influenced by fairness and support, and employees may not expect the same level of organizational support as in low-power-distance cultures.
- **Real-world examples**, such as Salesforce.com’s support for an employee with leukemia, illustrate how strong POS can contribute to a company’s positive reputation and employee satisfaction.

## Employee Engagement

- **Employee engagement** refers to the enthusiasm and emotional commitment employees have toward their work, going beyond job satisfaction and organizational commitment to include passion and devotion.
- Highly engaged employees are deeply connected to their organizations and contribute more energy and attention, while disengaged employees are physically present but mentally checked out, leading to significant productivity losses (estimated at $1.9 trillion annually).
- **Employee engagement is moderately related to both individual and organizational performance**, with research showing that organizations in the top quartile of engagement have 23% higher profitability, 18% higher productivity, and 64% fewer safety incidents compared to those in the bottom quartile.
- Teams with high engagement levels achieve higher customer satisfaction, greater productivity, increased profits, and lower turnover and accident rates.
- **Engagement levels vary globally**, with employees in the United States and Canada generally more engaged than those in Europe; regions with easier job markets tend to have fewer disengaged employees.
- **Organizations and managers can improve engagement** through interventions that increase autonomy, build resiliency, reduce or ease job demands, and support employee development (such as providing feedback and learning opportunities).
- Investing in engagement is especially important in high-turnover fields like education, where high burnout rates are common; fostering engagement can significantly reduce turnover and improve outcomes, such as student experiences in schools.

## Are These Job Attitudes All That Distinct?

- **Employee engagement is a widely discussed concept in organizations and consulting, but its definition is ambiguous and often overlaps with related constructs like psychological states, personality traits, and behaviors.**
- **There is debate about the usefulness of employee engagement because it is sometimes used as a catch-all term, and survey questions measuring engagement often resemble those for satisfaction, commitment, and involvement.**
- **Meta-analytic research shows a very strong relationship between employee engagement and other job attitudes, raising questions about whether these are truly distinct concepts.**
- **Despite definitional issues, employee engagement is generally found to predict important outcomes, such as high performance.**
- **Day-to-day variability in employee engagement is common, but employees with more consistent engagement tend to perform better than those whose engagement fluctuates significantly.**

## Job Satisfaction

- **There are two main approaches for measuring job satisfaction:** the single global rating method and the summation score method.
- **The single global rating method** asks individuals to respond to one overall question about their job satisfaction, providing a general sense of how satisfied they are.
- **The summation score method** breaks down job satisfaction into key elements (such as pay, promotion opportunities, supervision, and coworkers) and asks individuals to rate their satisfaction with each, then combines these ratings for an overall score.

## How Do You Measure Job Satisfaction?

- **Job satisfaction is a broad concept** that encompasses an employee’s positive feelings about their job, based on evaluating various job characteristics such as tasks, interactions, rules, hierarchies, performance standards, working conditions, and adaptation to technology.
- **Two main methods for measuring job satisfaction** are used: the single global rating (a one-question overall assessment, typically on a 1–5 scale) and the summation of job facets (rating multiple job elements like work type, skills, supervision, pay, promotion, culture, and coworker relationships, then combining these scores).
- **Both measurement methods are equally valid**, despite the summation of job facets seeming intuitively more accurate; the single global rating is quick and provides a general overview, while the summation method allows for identifying and addressing specific job-related issues.

## How Satisfied Are People in Their Jobs?

- **Most people report being satisfied with their jobs**, with 51% of U.S. employees describing themselves as extremely or very satisfied, and an additional 27% as somewhat satisfied.
- **Employees are generally more satisfied with interpersonal relationships at work** (such as with coworkers and supervisors) than with extrinsic factors like pay and promotion opportunities.
- **Job satisfaction is influenced by both internal organizational factors** (e.g., pay, benefits, social support) **and external conditions** (e.g., economic climate). For example, job satisfaction tends to increase during recessions and decrease during economic booms, likely due to fewer alternative job opportunities in tough economic times.
- **Income level affects job satisfaction**, with upper-income workers reporting higher overall satisfaction and greater satisfaction with specific aspects of their jobs (such as pay and promotion opportunities) compared to middle- and lower-income workers.

## What Causes Job Satisfaction?

- **Job satisfaction is influenced by several key characteristics, with job conditions being a primary factor.**
- **Job conditions** include aspects such as the nature of the work itself, the level of autonomy, the variety of tasks, opportunities for personal growth, and the physical work environment.
- **Different individuals may value different aspects** of their jobs, so the causes of job satisfaction can vary significantly from person to person.

## Job Conditions

- **Interesting jobs that offer training, variety, independence, and control are key contributors to employee satisfaction.**
- **Social factors such as interdependence, feedback, social support, and coworker interaction outside of work are strongly linked to job satisfaction, beyond just the nature of the work itself.**
- **Toxic work environments, including experiences of discrimination, lead to dissatisfaction and can negatively impact both physical and psychological health.**
- **Manager-employee relationships significantly influence job satisfaction, with the quality of these exchanges being more impactful in individualistic cultures (like Western countries) than in collectivistic cultures (like Asian countries).**
- **The importance of “fitting in” varies by region: in North America, fitting in with the organization and job is more important, while in East Asia, fitting in with the team or supervisor is more critical.**
- **Supportive work environments and strong organizational cultures, as seen in companies like Publix and Bain & Company, are associated with high employee satisfaction and positive performance standards.**
- **Overall, intrinsic job characteristics, social interactions, and quality supervision are major predictors of job satisfaction.**

## Personality and Individual Differences

- **Positive core self-evaluations (CSEs) are linked to higher job satisfaction**, as individuals who believe in their inner worth and competence tend to feel more satisfied at work, especially in collectivist cultures.
- **Intelligence contributes to job satisfaction**, since intelligent individuals often pursue complex jobs that fulfill their intellectual curiosity.
- **Person-job fit enhances satisfaction**, with people experiencing greater job satisfaction when their interests align with their job roles, as demonstrated by extensive research.

## Pay

- **Compensation is closely linked to job satisfaction**, with many people comparing their pay to that of peers or others in similar roles, which can impact how satisfied they feel at work.
- **Income does correlate with job satisfaction and happiness**, but this effect diminishes once a person reaches a comfortable standard of living; after that, increases in pay have less impact on overall happiness.
- **Discrepancies in pay compared to others can decrease job satisfaction**, but these effects are generally smaller for those who already have sufficient income.
- **Pay is often cited as the main reason for job dissatisfaction**, especially when pay raises do not keep up with the cost of living, or when employees feel their pay does not match the quality or quantity of their work.
- **Employees are more likely to be dissatisfied with wages than with benefits**, even though many undervalue the benefits they receive.
- **Employers can address dissatisfaction by offering robust benefits and clearly communicating their value**, as well as by explaining compensation strategies, though pay transparency alone does not always increase satisfaction.

## Outcomes of Job Satisfaction

- **Job satisfaction leads to improved job performance**, meaning when you are more satisfied with your job, you tend to perform better at work.
- **Organizational citizenship behavior increases with job satisfaction**, so you are more likely to go above and beyond your formal job duties when you are happy with your job.
- **Customer satisfaction is positively affected by job satisfaction**, as satisfied employees provide better service, leading to happier customers.
- **Life satisfaction is higher when job satisfaction is high**, indicating that being content at work contributes to your overall happiness and well-being.

## Job Performance

- **Happy workers are generally more productive**, as supported by a strong correlation found in a review of 300 studies.
- **Higher job satisfaction leads to better individual performance**, meaning that when you are more satisfied with your job, you tend to perform better.
- **Organizations with more satisfied employees are more effective overall** compared to those with fewer satisfied employees, highlighting the collective impact of job satisfaction on organizational success.

## Toward a Better World

- **Nvidia integrates social responsibility deeply into its organizational culture**, uniquely involving employees (Nvidians) in philanthropic and community-focused initiatives, which fosters strong organizational identification and empowerment.
- **Employee-driven programs are central to Nvidia’s approach**, including an employee-run philanthropic foundation and large-scale volunteer events like Project Inspire, which has evolved into Inspire 365, allowing employees to volunteer flexibly throughout the year.
- **Nvidia supports year-round community engagement** by offering gift matching and flextime for volunteering, enabling initiatives such as the Techsplorer program, where employees tutor and inspire children in artificial intelligence.
- **Nvidia’s rapid response to global disasters** includes organizing global campaigns and donations (e.g., over $1.9 million for earthquake relief in Turkey and Syria), as well as facilitating employee-led relief efforts.
- **These practices have a significant positive impact on employee attitudes**, with 97% of employees rating Nvidia as a great place to work, far exceeding the typical U.S. company average.
- **Research shows that when organizations align their actions with their stated values, as Nvidia does, employees experience greater respect, pride, and are more likely to engage in organizational citizenship behaviors (OCBs)**; conversely, a disconnect between stated values and actions can harm employee attitudes and identification.

## Organizational Citizenship Behavior (OCB)

- **Job satisfaction is moderately correlated with organizational citizenship behavior (OCB)**, meaning that employees who are more satisfied with their jobs are more likely to go beyond their formal job requirements, such as helping others and speaking positively about their organization.
- **Service organizations benefit from employee satisfaction**, as satisfied frontline employees (like airline ticket agents) tend to be friendlier and more responsive, which enhances customer satisfaction and loyalty.
- **Trust is a key mechanism linking job satisfaction to OCB**; when employees engage in OCB, managers respond with increased trust, and supportive coworker relationships further encourage helpful behaviors among employees.

## Customer Satisfaction

- **Employee satisfaction is directly linked to increased customer satisfaction and loyalty**, especially for employees who regularly interact with customers.
- **Companies that invest in employee well-being see positive customer outcomes**, as illustrated by Publix, which consistently ranks highly for both customer service and workplace quality.
- **Publix’s approach includes prioritizing employees’ financial, physical, and mental health**, reinforcing the idea that employee investment leads to better service and customer satisfaction.

## Life Satisfaction

- **Job satisfaction and life satisfaction are closely linked**, with changes in employment status impacting overall happiness.
- **Unemployment leads to decreased life satisfaction**, not solely due to loss of income, but also because work provides meaning and fulfillment.
- **Work is a significant part of life for most people**, and happiness in one's job contributes substantially to overall life satisfaction.

## The Impact of Job Dissatisfaction

- The exit–voice–loyalty–neglect framework identifies four main responses to job dissatisfaction: exit, voice, loyalty, and neglect, which differ based on whether they are active or passive, and constructive or destructive.
- **Exit** is an active, destructive response where you direct behavior toward leaving the job, such as searching for a new position or resigning, leading to individual or collective turnover and loss of organizational knowledge and skills.
- **Voice** is an active, constructive response where you attempt to improve conditions by suggesting changes, discussing issues with supervisors, or engaging in union activities.
- **Loyalty** is a passive, constructive response where you optimistically wait for conditions to improve, defend the organization against criticism, and trust management to resolve issues.
- **Neglect** is a passive, destructive response where you allow conditions to deteriorate, which may involve chronic absenteeism, lateness, reduced effort, or increased errors.
- Exit and neglect are associated with negative performance outcomes like lower productivity, higher absenteeism, and increased turnover, while voice and loyalty represent constructive ways to address dissatisfaction or tolerate unpleasant situations.

## Counterproductive Work Behavior (CWB)

- **Counterproductive work behavior (CWB)** includes actions like substance abuse, theft, excessive social media use, gossip, absenteeism, and tardiness, all of which harm organizations and are often rooted in negative attitudes, especially job dissatisfaction.
- **Job dissatisfaction is a key predictor of CWB**, leading to frustration, lower performance, and increased likelihood of deviant behaviors; this relationship may be stronger in men due to higher aggressiveness and lower impulse control.
- **Work environment norms influence CWB**—for example, high absenteeism within a team increases the likelihood of individual absenteeism, and abusive supervision can trigger a cycle of negative behaviors.
- **CWB manifests in diverse ways** depending on individual factors; employees may respond to dissatisfaction by quitting, misusing time, or taking supplies, so addressing only specific behaviors without tackling underlying dissatisfaction is ineffective.
- **Electronic monitoring does not consistently reduce CWB** and may harm employee–supervisor relationships; addressing the root cause of dissatisfaction is more effective.
- **CWB can be an emotional response to perceived unfairness**, as employees may use it to restore a sense of equity, raising complex ethical questions about what constitutes acceptable behavior.
- **Managers can mitigate CWB** by assessing employee attitudes, improving workplace conditions, measuring CWB (including self-assessments), fostering strong teams, integrating supervisors, formalizing policies, and using team-based incentives to reduce negative behavioral contagion.
- **Absenteeism is weakly related to job dissatisfaction**; it increases when alternative jobs are available and decreases when employees feel guilty about missing work.
- **Turnover is more strongly linked to job dissatisfaction** than absenteeism; dissatisfaction is the best predictor of turnover, and turnover can be contagious within dissatisfied groups, especially among high performers.
- **Alternative job prospects affect turnover**: when other jobs are available, employees are more likely to leave due to external "pull" factors rather than dissatisfaction "push" factors; high human capital increases the likelihood of turnover in dissatisfied employees.
- **Employee embeddedness (connections to job and community) reduces turnover**, especially in collectivist cultures, as employees are motivated to protect their acquired resources, but embeddedness in toxic environments can lead to negative health outcomes.

## Myth or Science?

- **Happy employees can contribute to higher profits**, as seen in successful companies like Hilton, Cisco, Marriott, and American Express, but not all sources of happiness lead to increased productivity or profitability.
- **Not all happiness-inducing perks are effective**; for example, extravagant amenities or traditional benefits like paid time off and health insurance do not guarantee higher job satisfaction, productivity, or profits.
- **Employee engagement is a key factor**—it reflects how involved and enthusiastic employees are at work, and higher engagement leads to harder work, better customer satisfaction, and lower turnover, all of which positively impact profits.
- **Low employee engagement is linked to poor organizational performance**, as shown by studies connecting high turnover from poor attitudes to decreased performance.
- **Employee engagement is currently low**, with only 31% of employees engaged, despite its proven importance for organizational success.
- **Organizations can improve engagement and performance** by selecting managers who can inspire and engage employees, and by training managers to set clear priorities, provide ongoing feedback, and create accountability.

## Managers Often “Don’t Get It”

- **Job satisfaction and morale significantly impact organizational performance**, as shown by companies with high employee morale experiencing stock price growth of 19.4%, compared to 10% for companies with medium or low morale.
- **Organizations recognized for employee satisfaction outperform the market**, with the “100 Best Companies to Work for in America” outperforming the stock market by a factor of 3.36 from 1992 to 2022.
- **Poor employee well-being leads to substantial financial losses**, with global turnover and lost productivity costs estimated at $322 billion.
- **Managers often overestimate employee satisfaction**, creating a gap between perceived and actual morale; for example, 86% of senior managers believed their organizations treated employees well, but only 55% of employees agreed.
- **This understanding gap is widespread and affects organizations of all sizes**, as seen in both U.S. and U.K. studies, and can negatively impact the bottom line.
- **Collecting and analyzing employee feedback is crucial**, as demonstrated by companies like Intuit, which created a dedicated role to gather and act on employee sentiment, helping to bridge the gap between management perceptions and employee realities.
- **Regular efforts to assess and improve job attitudes are necessary**, as surveys and other feedback mechanisms can help organizations better understand and address employee needs, ultimately supporting productivity and performance.
