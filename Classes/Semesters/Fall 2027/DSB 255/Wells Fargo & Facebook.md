## Summary: "Ethics at Wells Fargo and Facebook"

**Overview** The case study contrasts two very different companies — Wells Fargo (a massive, century-old banking institution) and Facebook/Meta (a much younger social media company) — that turned out to share two things: enormous scale and market value, and major ethics scandals in recent years.

### Wells Fargo: The Fake Accounts Scandal

- In 2013, the _LA Times_ exposed aggressive "cross-selling" practices at Wells Fargo branches, where employees opened accounts in customers' names without consent to hit unrealistic sales quotas.
- Investigations by the CFPB in 2016 confirmed employees had fraudulently opened over 1.5 million unauthorized accounts and 500,000 unauthorized credit cards between 2011–2016; the bank paid $185 million in fines.
- By 2018, the scope grew to 3.5 million unauthorized products, plus unneeded auto insurance and manipulated mortgage rates affecting hundreds of thousands of customers.
- The misconduct ran from tellers up through branch managers, driven by a culture of extreme sales targets, punishment for underperformance, and retaliation against whistleblowers.
- CEO John Stumpf initially blamed "dishonest" individual employees (5,300 were fired), denying any orchestrated scheme — but evidence showed leadership had cultivated this culture (Stumpf's "eight is great" mantra) and profited personally as his stock value rose over $200 million during the fraud period.
- Deeper analysis pointed to a broader shift in banking from a client-centered "fiduciary" model to a transactional, sales-driven model, plus red flags executives ignored: internal warnings, a training manual hinting the practice was known, and reports correlating cross-selling growth with unused accounts.
- Oversight failures compounded the problem: an ignored ethics hotline, a board claiming ignorance despite legal oversight duties, and the CEO also serving as board chair (limiting independent checks). A senior sales executive received a $125 million retirement payout right before the scandal broke.
- Multiple institutions responded — the CFPB, Comptroller of the Currency, U.S. Senate Banking Committee, and Federal Reserve (which took the unusual step of capping Wells Fargo's growth and forcing board changes) — though existing fraud, whistleblower, and compliance laws failed to prevent the misconduct in the first place.

### Facebook/Meta: Privacy and Misinformation Failures

- Facebook's ethical troubles trace back to its Harvard origins, when an early version was criticized for misusing private student data, followed by controversy over the News Feed and the Beacon ad system (which shared users' browsing/purchase data with advertisers). This led to a 2011 FTC settlement restricting Facebook's data use — which critics say changed little.
- In 2021, a public feud broke out between Facebook and Apple after Apple's iOS 14.5 update introduced "App Tracking Transparency," requiring apps to get explicit user consent before tracking their data. Apple framed this as protecting users whose personal information Facebook's ad-driven model depends on collecting and selling.
- Apple CEO Tim Cook publicly distinguished Apple's approach from Facebook's, calling privacy a human right and civil liberty, and distancing himself from the practices Zuckerberg's company faced criticism for — notably regarding the platform's role in 2016 U.S. election misinformation.
- Russian operatives were found to have used Facebook's ad-targeting tools and fake accounts/groups to spread disinformation and manipulate the 2016 election, leveraging ordinary users to spread content organically.
- Zuckerberg and COO Sheryl Sandberg were criticized for downplaying and slow-walking their response — Zuckerberg initially dismissed the idea that misinformation swayed the election as "pretty crazy" — and Facebook argued it couldn't reasonably police all content without becoming a "censor."
- Similar defensive responses followed criticism over COVID-19/vaccine misinformation on the platform.
- Facebook's credibility took a further hit in September 2021 when the _Wall Street Journal_ published a series based on leaked internal documents showing the company knew in detail about harms on its platforms — including issues like sex trafficking, drug cartels, election interference, hate speech, and harm to children — but often failed to act.
- The whistleblower, Frances Haugen (a former manager in Facebook's civic integrity division), went public in October 2021, testifying to the SEC, U.S. Congress, and European bodies that Facebook repeatedly chose its own financial interests over public good.
- Haugen wasn't the only internal critic: early investor Roger McNamee publicly criticized the company starting in 2018; co-founder Chris Hughes called for Facebook to be broken up in a 2019 op-ed; and data scientist Sophie Zhang revealed in 2020 that Facebook knew about foreign governments manipulating the platform but failed to address it.

### Core Theme

Both companies illustrate how growth-driven incentive structures, weak internal oversight, and leadership denial or minimization can allow large-scale ethical harm to persist — despite internal warnings, external criticism, and regulatory frameworks meant to catch it.
