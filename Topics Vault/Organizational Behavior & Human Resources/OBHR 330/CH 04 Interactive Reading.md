## Chapter 4

- **Emotions are intense feelings directed at someone or something, while moods are less intense feelings that often lack a specific stimulus and can last longer.**
- **Sources of emotions and moods include personality, day and time of the week, weather, stress, social activities, sleep, exercise, age, and gender.**
- **Emotional labor refers to the process by which employees manage their emotions to fulfill the emotional requirements of their job, which can lead to emotional exhaustion and reduced job satisfaction.**
- **Emotional intelligence (EI) is the ability to perceive, understand, manage, and regulate emotions in oneself and others, which can improve workplace relationships and performance.**
- **Emotion regulation strategies include cognitive reappraisal, suppression, social sharing, and mindfulness, which help individuals manage their emotional responses.**
- **Understanding emotions and moods is important for addressing organizational behavior (OB) issues such as motivation, leadership, communication, and decision-making.**

## Bringing Your Sense of Humor to Work

- **Southwest Airlines emphasizes a unique, employee-focused culture**, believing that treating employees well leads to better customer service and overall business success.
- **Core values at Southwest include humor and authenticity**, as seen in mottos like “Don’t take yourself too seriously” and “Don’t be a jerk,” and exemplified by founder Herb Kelleher’s leadership style.
- **Intangible assets like organizational culture and employee expression are seen as more valuable than tangible assets**, since competitors can easily replicate physical resources but not a distinctive culture.
- **Southwest employees are encouraged to express themselves and use humor**, resulting in memorable customer experiences, such as entertaining “gate games” during delays and comedic flight announcements.
- **The company’s approach has led to recognition for its positive workplace environment**, including awards and high rankings as an employer, especially for veterans.
- **Research supports that workplaces where employees can express their feelings are more creative, innovative, and productive**, and foster better relationships among employees, which boosts performance.
- **Recent financial challenges have led Southwest to announce job cuts, reduced hiring, and the suspension of employee celebrations**, raising concerns about the potential impact on its employee-centered culture and morale.
- **Emotions play a significant role in the workplace**, influencing both employee and customer experiences, and cannot be separated from work life.
- **Historically, organizational behavior (OB) research overlooked emotions**, viewing them as detrimental, but the ongoing “Affective Revolution” recognizes that emotions can be negative, neutral, or constructive, and are essential to understanding workplace behavior.

## What Are Emotions and Moods?

- **Emotions are intense, short-lived feelings typically triggered by a specific event**, such as anger from an insult or joy from good news.
- **Moods are longer-lasting, less intense states that often lack a clear cause**, like feeling generally positive or negative throughout the day.
- Emotions are more specific (e.g., anger, joy, fear), while moods are more general (e.g., positive or negative mood).
- The boundaries between emotions and moods can blur; for example, a strong emotion can influence your mood for hours, and your mood can make you more likely to experience certain emotions.
- Positive emotions can enhance productivity and well-being, as shown by research linking happiness to a 13% increase in worker productivity.
- Both emotions and moods significantly impact attitudes, decision making, and behavior, and can have both positive and negative effects in personal and professional contexts.

## Positive and Negative Affect

- **Emotions in the workplace are classified as positive or negative:** Positive emotions (like joy and gratitude) reflect favorable feelings, while negative emotions (like anger and guilt) reflect unfavorable feelings.
- **Positive affect (PA) and negative affect (NA) are dimensions of mood:** High PA includes emotions such as excitement, enthusiasm, and elation, indicating a good general mood. High NA includes emotions such as nervousness, stress, and anxiety, indicating a bad general mood.
- **Positive and negative emotions or moods can co-occur:** You can experience high PA and high NA simultaneously, such as feeling both tense (negative) and excited (positive).
- **Boredom is the absence of both PA and NA:** Employees often suppress boredom to complete tasks, which can lead to mind-wandering and reduced productivity.
- **Completing meaningful tasks after boredom can prevent negative effects:** Finishing a meaningful task after feeling bored helps stop boredom from negatively affecting future productivity.

## The Basic Emotions

- There are many emotions, but six are widely considered universal: anger, fear, sadness, happiness, disgust, and surprise.
- These universal emotions are commonly experienced in the workplace, often triggered by events such as performance reviews, and can lead to lingering negative moods if not managed.
- Recognizing emotions in others is challenging due to the complexity of facial expressions, personal emotional biases, and cultural differences.
- People generally recognize emotions more accurately within their own cultural group, both in facial and vocal expressions, and there is evidence of racial biases in emotion recognition.
- Artificial intelligence is being used to analyze vocal expressions of emotion, revealing cultural differences in how emotions are expressed and perceived, but its effectiveness with facial expressions is still uncertain.
- Understanding and adapting to cultural differences in emotional expression is crucial for effective communication in diverse and globalized workplaces.

## Moral Emotions

- **Moral emotions are closely linked to your instant judgments about right and wrong in a situation, influencing how you respond to events with moral implications.** For example, witnessing unethical behavior like a sexist or racist slur can evoke emotions such as disgust, anger, or contempt based on your moral evaluation.
- **Moral emotions differ from other emotions in their triggers and responses.** You are more likely to confront situations that evoke moral anger than those that evoke general anger. Examples include feeling sympathy for others’ suffering, guilt for your own immoral actions, or anger at injustice.
- **Moral emotions are shaped by context, learned norms, and cultural differences.** Children develop these emotions as they learn societal standards, and what is considered moral can vary across cultures, affecting the emotions experienced.
- **Ethical decision making is influenced more by emotions than by logical reasoning.** Although people often believe ethical choices are rational, research shows that feelings play a primary role, and group membership can shape your perceptions of what is ethical.
- **People tend to judge outgroup members more harshly for moral transgressions and are more lenient toward ingroup members, leading to double standards in ethical judgments.**
- **Emotional reactions often prompt ethical actions, such as helping others, apologizing, or intervening in unfair situations, highlighting the role of emotions in ethical behavior.**
- **Emotions are typically short-lived, while moods are longer-lasting, and understanding their impact is important for organizational behavior.**

## Experiencing Moods and Emotions

- **Most people experience positive moods more frequently than negative moods**, a phenomenon known as the positivity offset, where individuals tend to feel mildly positive even when nothing specific is happening.
- **Negative emotions are more salient and memorable than positive ones**, as people dwell on negative events much longer, making negative moods more noticeable despite the overall prevalence of positive moods.
- **Cultural differences influence how emotions are valued and experienced**; for example, cultures like Mexico and Brazil emphasize positive affect, while cultures such as Japan, South Korea, and China view negative emotions as useful and are less motivated to suppress them.
- **Negative emotions have less harmful effects on health in Eastern cultures compared to Western cultures**, possibly because valuing negative affect helps individuals accept circumstances and cope more effectively.
- **Cultural preferences for emotional expression impact workplace dynamics**, as seen in studies where European Americans displayed and valued positive affect more in job applications and interviews than Hong Kong Chinese, influencing hiring decisions and perceptions of ideal candidates.
- **Despite cultural differences, people worldwide interpret and desire positive and negative emotions in similar ways**, but the emphasis placed on certain emotions can shape individual experiences and workplace diversity outcomes.

## The Function of Emotions

- **Positive emotions in the workplace enhance job attitudes, engagement, performance, and organizational citizenship behavior (OCB), while reducing withdrawal and counterproductive work behaviors (CWBs).**
- **Individuals with a tendency toward positive affect as a personality trait experience more long-term positive outcomes.**
- **Emotions are not inherently irrational; they provide valuable information that helps guide understanding and behavior.**
- **Experiencing and recognizing emotions can actually make thinking more rational by informing decision-making and perception.**
- **People in a negative mood may be better at distinguishing truthful from inaccurate information compared to those in a happy mood.**
- **Acknowledging and understanding both your own emotions and those of others improves your ability to predict behavior and manage interactions effectively.**

## Sources of Emotions and Moods

- **Emotions and moods are influenced by multiple sources**, not just immediate events or interactions.
- **Personal interactions**, such as a negative response from a coworker, can trigger emotional reactions and affect your mood.
- **Physical states or needs**, like lack of coffee or sleep, can also impact how you feel and respond emotionally.
- **Emotions and moods can affect work performance and team dynamics**, as negative feelings may distract you or harm collaboration.
- **It's important to recognize that emotions and moods may have deeper or multiple origins**, beyond the most obvious or immediate cause.

## Personality

- **Affect has a personality trait component**, meaning individuals have inherent tendencies to experience certain moods and emotions more frequently than others.
- **Affect intensity** refers to how strongly a person feels emotions; some people experience both positive and negative emotions more deeply than others.
- The **tendency to experience positive or negative emotions** and the **intensity of those emotions** influence important workplace factors, including typical moods, job satisfaction, and engagement.
- Research indicates that the **tendency to experience positive emotions and moods is the strongest predictor of employee engagement**.

## Time of Day

- **Moods fluctuate throughout the day:** Positive affect (PA) typically rises after sunrise, peaks in mid to late morning, remains steady until evening, and then declines until the next sunrise. Negative affect (NA) is lowest early in the morning and increases throughout the day, peaking late in the evening.
- **Social media data supports mood patterns:** Analysis of tweets shows that positive sentiments are most common during daylight hours, while negative sentiments are more frequent late at night.
- **Time of day influences work behavior:** Morning people are more creative and empathetic in the morning, leading to increased prosocial behaviors early in the day. Evening people show higher creativity and better moods at night, and their prosocial behaviors are more consistent throughout the workday.

## Day of the Week

- **Mood patterns fluctuate throughout the week, with positive affect (PA) typically peaking on Fridays, Saturdays, and Sundays, and reaching its lowest point on Mondays for U.S. adults and adolescents.**
- **Negative affect (NA) tends to be highest in the early to middle part of the week (Tuesdays–Thursdays) across most cultures.**
- **These weekly mood trends are observed in several cultures, but not universally; for example, in Japan, PA is higher on Monday than on Friday or Saturday.**
- **The findings are based on studies of both adolescents and adults, showing similar patterns, but individual differences and other factors also influence mood.**

## Weather

- **Weather has little actual effect on mood for most people**, despite common beliefs that mood is tied to weather conditions.
- **Illusory correlation** is the tendency to associate two unrelated events, such as weather and mood, leading people to mistakenly believe that weather significantly influences how they feel.
- Many people identify as “rain haters” or “summer lovers,” but these self-perceptions are not strongly supported by scientific evidence.

## Stress

- **Stressful events at work negatively impact your emotions and moods**, with examples including negative emails, tight deadlines, lost sales, or criticism from a manager.
- **Continuous exposure to even low-level stress can lead to increasing strain over time**, gradually worsening your mood and increasing negative emotions.
- While some people may temporarily thrive under stress, **chronic or intense stress typically harms mood and emotional well-being**.
- **Highly stressful and emotionally charged situations often trigger a natural tendency to disengage** as a coping mechanism.

## Social Interactions

- **Negative interactions with others can significantly impact your emotions and mood**, which in turn can influence your relationships, either strengthening or weakening them.
- **Emotions from workplace interactions can spill over into your personal life**, affecting relationships with family members, not just coworkers.
- **Social interactions are not always clearly positive or negative**; neutral or unremarkable interactions can lead to boredom and reduced motivation to maintain relationships, while mixed interactions can create feelings of conflict or bittersweetness.
- **Emotions, especially negative ones, can spread collectively within groups or organizations**, often amplifying and spreading more quickly than positive emotions.
- **Leaders play a key role in managing the spread of negative emotions** by helping employees reframe or reappraise their experiences, which can reduce the harmful impact of these emotions.

## Sleep

- **About one-third of U.S. adults do not get enough sleep daily, and 50 to 70 million Americans suffer from chronic sleep disorders.**
- **Poor sleep quality negatively impacts mood, decision making, and emotional control, increasing risks for disease, injury, anxiety, and depression.**
- **Insufficient sleep can impair job performance and satisfaction.**
- **Regular, increased sleep helps reduce the negative effects of fatigue and stress on emotions.**

## Exercise

- **Exercise is effective in improving mood and managing negative emotions**, helping you recover from negative experiences.
- **Physical activity can reduce symptoms of depression, anxiety, and distress**, providing mental health benefits beyond just physical health.
- **Regular exercise enhances task focus and overall performance at work**, supporting both emotional well-being and productivity.

## Gender Identity

- **Gender differences in emotional expression exist but are often small or trivial**, and may be influenced more by socialization, stereotypes, and measurement methods than by inherent differences.
- **Men are socialized to control emotional expressivity more than women**, leading to harsher judgments of emotional men and feelings of shame when expressing emotions openly.
- **Women of color, particularly Black women, face discrimination based on emotional display**, with the "angry Black woman" stereotype causing observers to attribute their anger to personality traits rather than situational factors.
- **Stereotypical perceptions about gender and emotion persist in the workplace**, despite limited evidence supporting these stereotypes.
- **Exercise is linked to improved mood and productivity in the workplace**, as seen in companies offering physical activity opportunities to employees.
- **Understanding emotions and moods is important for developing effective workplace coping strategies**, such as emotional labor.

## Emotional Labor

- **Emotional labor refers to the requirement for employees to display specific emotions that are desired by their organization during interactions at work.**
- **This type of labor is distinct from physical and mental labor, as it involves managing and sometimes faking emotions to meet job expectations.**
- **Emotional labor is crucial for effective job performance, especially in roles involving customer service or interpersonal transactions.**
- **Examples include flight attendants being expected to be cheerful, funeral directors to appear sad, and doctors to maintain emotional neutrality, regardless of their true feelings.**

## Controlling Emotional Displays

- **Felt emotions** are your actual, internal emotional experiences, while **displayed emotions** are those you show because your organization expects them, often regardless of how you truly feel.
- Many workplaces require employees to display positive emotions (like happiness) and suppress negative ones (like anger or fear), especially in interactions with customers or supervisors.
- **Display rules** are explicit or implicit guidelines about which emotions you should show at work; these rules are often stricter in customer-facing roles and can vary in intensity.
- Employees with **moderate display rules** and more discretion tend to perform better in sales than those with very high or very low display rules.
- **Informal display rules** can be biased; for example, Black workers may be expected to display more positive emotions due to stereotypes, leading to greater emotional labor compared to white workers.
- **Gender differences** exist in emotional display expectations: women often feel more pressure to control their emotional expressions, while men believe it is more important for themselves and other men to do so.
- **Surface acting** involves faking or suppressing emotions to meet display rules (e.g., smiling when you don’t feel happy), while **deep acting** means trying to actually change your internal feelings to match required displays.
- Surface acting is linked to increased stress, lower job satisfaction, emotional exhaustion, work–family conflict, absenteeism, and insomnia.
- Deep acting is associated with higher job satisfaction, better job performance, improved well-being, and more positive customer interactions.
- **Emotional dissonance** is the conflict between felt and displayed emotions; chronic emotional dissonance can lead to burnout, reduced job performance, lower satisfaction, and negative behaviors like substance abuse or aggression toward coworkers.

## Myth or Science?

- **All employees experience emotional labor, but the degree and consequences vary significantly based on individual differences such as personality.**
- **Introverts find emotional labor, especially surface acting (hiding true feelings), more exhausting and are more likely to experience negative emotions compared to extraverts.**
- **Deep acting (modifying true feelings) is more strongly linked to negative emotions for extraverts, indicating both positive and negative emotional impacts for them.**
- **Extraverts tend to perform better in service and sales roles due to their sociability and greater ability to handle emotional demands, but surface acting still leads to emotional exhaustion and lower job satisfaction for all employees.**
- **Organizations can help by teaching employees to view customer demands as challenges rather than threats, reducing the gap between expected and felt emotions.**
- **Personality is not the only factor; race also influences emotional labor. Black service providers often need to exert more emotional effort to be perceived as warm due to racial stereotypes, leading to greater emotional demands and negative impacts on well-being.**
- **Supervisors have rated Black employees lower on interpersonal warmth and job performance, highlighting inequities and the need for strategies to reduce barriers faced by Black employees, especially in service industries.**
- **Training can help employees, leaders, and managers learn to regulate emotional displays, regardless of individual differences.**

## Emotional Intelligence

- **Emotional intelligence (EI) is the ability to perceive, understand, and regulate emotions in yourself and others.**
- EI is linked to improved job attitudes, academic and job performance, teamwork effectiveness, organizational citizenship behaviors (OCBs), and better career decision-making, while reducing counterproductive work behaviors (CWBs).
- Programs like Google’s “Search Inside Yourself” (SIY) demonstrate the practical value of EI, helping employees manage workplace relationships and personal stress.
- EI scores tend to increase from individual contributor roles to middle management, with middle managers having the highest EI due to the interpersonal demands of their roles; CEOs, on average, have the lowest EI scores.
- High EI in leaders and employees is associated with greater popularity, success, and vigilant leadership, which enhances teamwork proactivity and proficiency.
- There are limits to EI’s effectiveness: self-focused EI can reduce stress but may have physiological costs, and other-focused EI only weakly predicts task performance, especially in emotionally demanding situations.

## An Ethical Choice

- **There is no universally accepted Emotional Intelligence (EI) test**, and different tests (like MSCEIT, Trait EI Questionnaire, SJT of EI) may yield varying results across cultures, indicating that EI assessments may need cultural adaptation.
- **Applicants may perceive EI tests as unfair or biased**, especially if the tests include culturally specific elements (such as face recognition tasks with non-diverse images), and those who score lower may view the hiring process negatively.
- **EI tests are not equally predictive for all job types**; they tend to be more valid for roles requiring significant social interaction (e.g., salespeople, public servants, CEOs), and may need to be tailored or omitted for other positions.
- **The constructs measured by EI tests are not always clear**, as they may overlap with personality or general intelligence, which can be assessed through other established measures.
- **There is insufficient research on the relationship between EI and workplace behaviors like counterproductive work behavior (CWB)**, and some evidence suggests high EI could facilitate both detecting and committing deception.
- **Managers should be cautious and ethical when using EI tests in hiring**, considering the limitations, potential biases, and the specific requirements of the job.
- **Key unresolved questions include:** what aspect of EI is being measured (general EI, emotion regulation, understanding, or perception), the reliability and best methods for EI testing, and whether EI can be developed through training.
- **Research suggests EI can be trained and developed as a skill**, though those who might benefit most from EI training are often least likely to pursue it.
- **Despite ongoing debates and limitations, EI has some support in organizational behavior research as a factor in job performance**, making it a consideration for managers, but not a definitive tool for selection decisions.

## Emotion Regulation

- **Emotion regulation involves identifying and modifying your emotions** to influence how you feel and behave.
- **Emotion regulation is important for job performance and organizational citizenship behavior (OCB)**, meaning your ability to manage emotions can impact how well you perform tasks and contribute positively at work.
- **Individuals differ in their natural tendency to use emotion regulation**, and understanding who uses these strategies and when is important for workplace effectiveness.

## Emotion Regulation Influences and Outcomes

- **Individuals high in neuroticism have more difficulty regulating their emotions and often feel their moods are uncontrollable.**
- **People with lower self-esteem are less likely to attempt to improve sad moods, possibly due to believing they do not deserve to feel better.**
- **Workplace diversity increases the likelihood of emotion regulation; younger employees regulate emotions more in the presence of older colleagues, and underrepresented racial groups regulate emotions more in less diverse environments to fit in.**
- **Diversity in work groups can lead to more conscious and effective emotion regulation among members.**
- **Regulating emotions requires effort and can be exhausting, sometimes intensifying the emotion you are trying to change (e.g., focusing on fear can increase fear).**
- **Seeking positive emotional experiences (like having a pleasant conversation) is more effective for achieving positive moods than simply avoiding negative experiences (like avoiding a hostile coworker).**

## Emotion Regulation Techniques

- **Emotional suppression** involves holding back initial emotional responses, which can help with practical thinking during crises but is generally less effective than expressing emotions and can negatively impact mental health, emotional ability, physical health, and relationships if used regularly.
- Suppression is most beneficial in crisis situations (e.g., a portfolio manager suppressing panic during a market drop) but is linked to increased depression and anxiety when used as a daily strategy.
- **Cognitive reappraisal** means reframing how you view an emotional situation, which is especially effective when stressors are uncontrollable (e.g., seeing job loss as an opportunity). This technique improves cognitive flexibility and creativity and is more likely to be used by those who see emotions as controllable and helpful.
- **Social sharing (venting)**, or openly expressing emotions to others, can help regulate emotions and reduce anger, especially when the listener responds supportively. However, venting to unsympathetic listeners or to the person who caused the distress can worsen feelings or escalate negativity.
- The effectiveness of emotion regulation strategies varies: suppression is associated with greater emotional exhaustion and withdrawal at work, while cognitive reappraisal is linked to better coping and less exhaustion, highlighting the importance of choosing effective regulation techniques.

## Ethics of Emotion Regulation

- **Emotion regulation involves ethical considerations that exist on a spectrum**, from viewing emotional control as unethical "acting" to advocating for complete emotional control for objectivity.
- **Different motivations for regulating emotions have ethical pros and cons**, such as maintaining professionalism versus concealing true thoughts.
- **Research suggests that "acting" happy can actually improve your mood**, as demonstrated by participants who acted happy during interactions and subsequently felt better.
- **Adaptiveness is crucial in emotion regulation**—being aware of your emotions and choosing the most appropriate regulation strategy for each situation leads to better outcomes.

## OB Applications of Emotions and Moods

- **Emotions and moods influence key organizational behavior (OB) areas** such as selection processes, decision making, creativity, motivation, leadership, negotiation, customer service, work–life conflict, unethical workplace behaviors, and safety.
- **Emotional states can impact decision quality and creativity**, affecting how you solve problems and generate new ideas.
- **Moods and emotions play a role in motivation and leadership**, shaping how you respond to leaders and how leaders inspire others.
- **Negotiation outcomes and customer service interactions are affected by emotions**, influencing both the process and satisfaction of those involved.
- **Work–life conflict, unethical behaviors, and safety are linked to emotional experiences**, as negative emotions can increase conflict, unethical actions, and safety risks.

## The Selection Process

- **Emotional intelligence (EI) is a valuable trait for jobs requiring social interaction**, as high EI is linked to better job performance, demonstrated by U.S. Air Force recruiters who were 2.6 times more successful when selected for high EI.
- **Applicant emotions influence hiring decisions**, both consciously and unconsciously, with positive emotional displays often leading to favorable selection outcomes.
- **Positive moods among team members can enhance the work environment**, as emotions can be contagious; charismatic leaders, for example, can transfer their positive emotions to followers, fostering a more positive team dynamic.
- **Job seekers benefit from self-compassion**, as it helps buffer against negative emotions and setbacks commonly experienced during the job search process.

## Decision Making

- **Positive emotions and moods improve decision making and problem-solving abilities**, leading you to find better solutions.
- **Negative emotions and moods have complex effects on decision making**; for example, depression and worry can impair your decision-making, while anger may lead you to make stronger but not necessarily better choices.
- **Negative moods can sometimes enhance originality in choices**, showing that both positive and negative emotions influence your decisions, though the exact effects depend on additional variables.

## Creativity

- **Creativity in the workplace is influenced by emotions and moods**, with research indicating that people in good moods tend to generate more original ideas and options, likely due to increased flexibility and openness in their thinking.
- **There are conflicting views on the impact of positive moods on creativity**: some argue that positive moods enhance creativity, while others suggest that they may cause relaxation and reduce critical thinking, potentially lowering creativity; individuals who worry more may sometimes perform better on creative tasks.
- **A more nuanced perspective considers moods as activating or deactivating**: activating moods (such as anger, fear, or elation) increase creativity regardless of being positive or negative, while deactivating moods (like sorrow, depression, or serenity) decrease creativity; emotional intelligence, exemplified by leaders like Dan Campbell, enables effective decision-making and management of emotions in high-pressure situations.

## Motivation

- **Moods and emotions significantly impact motivation**, with positive moods leading to increased helpfulness, self-esteem, and superior job performance.
- **Performance feedback affects mood**, which in turn influences motivation; positive feedback can create an upward spiral of creativity, positive mood, and improved performance.
- **Managers can enhance employee motivation and performance by fostering positive moods** in the workplace, as this encourages a cycle of beneficial behaviors and outcomes.

## Leadership

- **Leaders who promote positive emotions, such as optimism and enthusiasm, foster greater cooperation, creativity, and positive social interactions among employees and customers.**
- **Humor from leaders can elevate followers’ moods and act as a social lubricant, but it is effective only when genuinely shared and perceived as funny.**
- **Displays of sadness by leaders can enhance followers’ analytic performance, possibly by increasing their focus on tasks to provide support.**
- **Sharing negative emotions like anger can improve team performance if expressed at moderate levels, but excessive negativity can have adverse effects, such as increased anxiety, unethical behavior, reduced communication, or employee turnover.**
- **Emotional content in leadership communication is crucial for gaining employee buy-in for organizational vision and change, especially when goals are vague or distant, as it helps employees feel connected and more willing to accept change.**

## Negotiation

- **Emotions, particularly anger, can influence negotiation outcomes**; expressing anger may lead opponents to believe you have reached your limit, prompting them to concede.
- **Anger can sometimes prompt both parties to make better concessions** if it leads the opponent to seek the cause of the anger.
- **Using anger is risky when you have less information or power**, as it can result in worse outcomes, especially if you refuse to consider the opponent’s perspective.
- **Rudeness during negotiation induces a negative mood in the other party**, increasing their anchoring and making them less likely to compromise.
- **The effectiveness of displaying negative emotions depends on context**; while it can sometimes be strategic, actually feeling negative emotions like anger, regret, or pride about your performance can harm your results in future negotiations.
- **Poor negotiation outcomes lead to negative emotions and perceptions**, reducing willingness to share information or cooperate in subsequent negotiations.

## Customer Service

- **Workers’ emotional states directly impact the quality of customer service, influencing customer satisfaction and repeat business through emotional contagion.**
- **Positive emotions from employees, such as smiling or sharing humor, lead to positive customer responses, increased satisfaction, loyalty, trust, and longer shopping durations, provided customers perceive these emotions as genuine and appropriate.**
- **Negative interactions, such as employees feeling unfairly treated by customers, make it harder for employees to display expected positive emotions, leading to emotional dissonance that can harm both employees and the organization.**
- **Managers can reduce negative emotional contagion by fostering psychological safety, allowing employees to express feelings and make mistakes without fear, and avoiding micromanagement, which signals distrust.**

## Toward a Better World

- **CSR messaging is essential for connecting with customers and building loyalty**, rather than relying solely on actions or assuming public indifference; effectively communicating CSR efforts can convince stakeholders of their importance and drive positive change.
- **Scream Agency exemplifies a strategic, emotion-driven approach to CSR communication**, focusing on being seen, heard, and good, and using emotional intelligence to connect with both customers and employees.
- Scream’s four-step process includes: (1) identifying social problems, (2) preparing stakeholder messaging, (3) convincing stakeholders of solutions, and (4) emotionally engaging the audience.
- **Research supports the impact of CSR messaging**: marketing and CSR professionals shape public opinion, internal messaging can reduce employee stress, CSR increases employee pride and retention, and positive CSR communication fosters favorable emotions and identification among customers.
- **Challenges exist in managing emotions in CSR**: rational business cases can undermine moral motivation among managers, emotions are complex and vary among individuals, and not all stakeholders will interpret or respond to CSR efforts in the same way.
- **An adaptive approach to emotional management in CSR messaging is necessary** to address diverse reactions and maximize the effectiveness of CSR initiatives among employees, managers, and customers.

## Work–Life Conflict

- **Moods at work and home are interconnected**, with events in one domain influencing your mood in the other; a good day at work often leads to a better mood at home, and vice versa.
- **Positive moods at work can spill over into off-work hours**, while negative moods at work can be improved after taking a break, showing the restorative power of downtime.
- **Pleasurable recovery activities after work enhance mood the following day**, and for leaders, this improved mood can positively impact the moods of their followers.

## Unethical Workplace Behaviors

- **Counterproductive work behaviors (CWBs) are unethical or deviant actions that harm organizations and their members.** These behaviors often stem from negative emotions and can range from minor acts like gossiping or excessive internet use to more serious actions such as undermining colleagues or taking undue credit.
- **Envy and anger are key negative emotions that drive CWBs.** Envy arises when you desire something another person has, leading to malicious behaviors like sabotaging others or claiming their achievements. Anger can cause you to blame others, interpret their actions as hostile, and act aggressively, either verbally or physically.
- **Managers need to monitor employee emotions to prevent CWBs, but privacy concerns exist.** While tracking emotions through surveys or digital communication analysis can help identify issues, many employees view such monitoring as intrusive.

## Safety and Injury at Work

- **Negative moods increase workplace injury risk** by making individuals more anxious and less able to cope with hazards effectively.
- **Anxiety and fear can lead to pessimism** about safety measures, causing individuals to believe precautions are ineffective or to panic or freeze in dangerous situations.
- **Negative moods heighten distractibility**, which can result in careless behaviors and accidents.
- **Feelings of homesickness and negative affect** have been shown in studies to predict poorer job performance and reduced safety behaviors among both migrant workers and expatriate military personnel.
- **Employers can enhance safety and reduce costs** by ensuring that workers in bad moods are not assigned to potentially dangerous tasks.
