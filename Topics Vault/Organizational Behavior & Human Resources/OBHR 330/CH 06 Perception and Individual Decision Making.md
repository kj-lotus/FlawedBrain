## Chapter 6

- **Perception is influenced by factors such as the perceiver’s attitudes, motives, interests, experience, and expectations, as well as the target’s characteristics and the context of the situation.**
- **Attribution theory explains how you interpret others’ behavior by attributing it to internal or external causes, often using shortcuts like the fundamental attribution error and self-serving bias.**
- **Perception directly impacts decision making because your interpretation of information shapes the choices you consider and the actions you take.**
- **Decision making in organizations is guided by models such as rational decision making, bounded rationality, and intuition, but is often affected by biases like overconfidence, anchoring, confirmation, and availability.**
- **Individual differences (such as personality, gender, and mental ability) and organizational constraints (like performance evaluations, reward systems, and time pressures) can significantly affect how decisions are made.**
- **Ethical decision making can be evaluated using three main criteria: utilitarianism (greatest good for the greatest number), rights (respecting individual rights), and justice (fairness and equity).**
- **Creativity in organizations follows a three-stage model: idea generation, idea evaluation, and idea implementation.**

## Tough Decisions in Times of Crisis

- **Effective crisis leadership involves separating strategic oversight from tactical decisions**, as demonstrated by Jean-Claude Gallet, who delegated tactical control to his deputy to maintain a broader strategic focus during the Notre-Dame fire.
- **Prioritization and goal setting are crucial in high-pressure situations**; Gallet chose to let some parts of the cathedral burn in order to concentrate resources on saving the towers and preserving key artifacts, despite external criticism.
- **Clear and transparent communication is essential for successful crisis management**, both within the team and with external stakeholders, such as government officials and the public, to explain decisions and manage expectations.
- **External pressures can challenge decision making**, but maintaining confidence in the team and sticking to well-defined goals can lead to successful outcomes even under intense scrutiny.
- **Decision making is influenced by perception, which can be subject to error and bias**, highlighting the importance of understanding how perceptions shape choices in both individual and organizational contexts.

## Factors That Influence Perception

- **Perception is the process by which you organize and interpret sensory information to give meaning to your environment, and it can differ significantly from objective reality.**
- **Your perceptions, rather than objective facts, drive your behavior and decisions, making perception a central concept in organizational behavior.**
- **Perception can be influenced and distorted by several factors, which are grouped into three main categories:**
    - **Factors in the perceiver:** These include your attitudes, motives, interests, past experiences, and expectations, all of which shape how you interpret information.
    - **Factors in the target:** Characteristics of the person or object being perceived, such as novelty, motion, sounds, size, background, and similarity to others, can affect your perception.
    - **Factors in the situation:** The context or environment, including time, work setting, and social situation, also impacts how you perceive things.
- **Stereotyping and biases, such as weight bias, can lead to unfair judgments and discrimination, especially in workplace settings, by activating unfounded assumptions about individuals.**
- **Initial impressions, especially about moral character, are often formed quickly and may be based on incomplete or inaccurate information.**

## Perceiver

- **Your perception of a target is shaped by your personal characteristics**, including attitudes, personality, motives, interests, values, past experiences, and expectations, which can lead you to interpret information in ways that align with your own thinking rather than objective reality.
- **Biases in perception can manifest in workplace evaluations**, such as supervisors viewing early-starting employees as more conscientious, unless the supervisor shares similar habits (e.g., being a night owl), which reduces this bias.
- **Power and status affect responses to workplace incivility**, with lower-status individuals more likely to avoid confrontation and offer only social support, while higher-status individuals may directly confront perpetrators; leaders may also confuse their recollections with actual events, leading to reliance on inaccurate beliefs or actions.

## Target

- **Perception is influenced by the relationship between a target and its background**, as well as the tendency to group together things that are nearby or similar.
- **Bias can occur when more attention is given to certain individuals based on characteristics like race**, which can lead to unequal outcomes, such as greater diagnostic accuracy for white patients compared to Black patients.
- **Implicit stereotypes can form when people assume that members of a group with distinguishable characteristics are alike in other, unrelated ways**, which can result in harmful prejudgments, such as discrimination against people with criminal records even if they were wrongly arrested.
- **Certain target characteristics, like a pleasant appearance, can positively influence outcomes**, as shown in microloan transactions where more attractive customers were more likely to receive and quickly obtain loan approval.
- **Machine learning techniques have demonstrated that pleasant appearance can predict loan approval with about 60% accuracy**, highlighting the impact of appearance-based biases in decision-making processes.

## Context

- **Context and situational factors significantly influence perception**, including timing, location, environmental conditions (like light and heat), and the specific situation.
- **Physical cues, such as the state of your environment, affect how others perceive you**; for example, a messy office may lead others to view you as less conscientious, more disagreeable, and more anxious compared to a tidy office.
- **Group membership impacts perception**, with the perceiver's group membership having a stronger effect than the target's; team norms create strong situations that shape how you judge others' behaviors and performance.
- **People are often unaware of the factors shaping their perceptions and may lack insight into their own abilities**, leading to distorted views of reality.
- **Increased self-awareness and objective measurement can help reduce perceptual distortions**; for example, recognizing personal biases (such as racial biases) can motivate individuals to control prejudices and become more aware of their own biases.

## Person Perception: Making Judgments About Others

- **Attribution theory explains how you determine the causes of others’ behavior, attributing actions either to internal factors (like personality or ability) or external factors (like the situation or environment).**
- **Your perceptions of others are often shaped by first impressions and minimal cues, which may not accurately reflect reality.**
- **Common shortcuts in evaluating others include relying on limited evidence and making quick judgments, which can lead to errors in understanding the true reasons behind someone’s actions.**

## Attribution Theory

- **Attribution theory explains how you judge others' behavior by assigning meaning to their actions, often distinguishing between internal (personal) and external (situational) causes.**
- **Internal causation** is when you believe behavior is due to personal factors (e.g., laziness), while **external causation** is when you attribute behavior to outside circumstances (e.g., bad traffic). There is also **relational attribution**, where behavior is explained by the relationship between people (e.g., performance attributed to working with a star employee).
- **Three key factors influence whether you make an internal or external attribution:**
    - **Distinctiveness:** Does the person behave differently in different situations? High distinctiveness (behavior is unusual for the person) leads to external attribution; low distinctiveness (behavior is typical) leads to internal attribution.
    - **Consensus:** Do others behave similarly in the same situation? High consensus (others act the same) leads to external attribution; low consensus (others act differently) leads to internal attribution.
    - **Consistency:** Does the person behave the same way over time? High consistency (behavior is repeated) leads to internal attribution; low consistency (behavior is rare) leads to external attribution.
- **Fundamental attribution error** is the tendency to overestimate internal factors and underestimate external factors when judging others’ behavior, which can lead to unfair assessments (e.g., assuming poor performance is due to lack of effort rather than external challenges).
- **Self-serving bias** is when you attribute your successes to internal factors and your failures to external factors. This bias is stronger in individuals who value achievement and can affect leaders’ behavior and organizational outcomes.
- **Cultural differences affect attribution:** Collectivist cultures (e.g., China) are more likely to make external or group-based attributions, while individualist cultures (e.g., the United States) focus on internal or individual attributions. This influences how blame and praise are assigned in different societies.
- **Attribution errors and biases can impact workplace dynamics, such as supervision, performance evaluation, and interpersonal relationships.**

## Toward a Better World

- **Greenwashing** refers to organizations misrepresenting their environmental practices to appear more socially responsible than they actually are, often to gain market share or improve public perception.
- Volkswagen’s “Dieselgate” scandal is a key example, where the company installed software (“defeat devices”) in over 11 million vehicles to falsify emissions test results, making cars appear compliant with environmental standards while actually emitting up to 40 times the legal pollutant limit.
- The defeat device software could detect testing conditions and alter engine performance to pass emissions tests, but would revert to higher-emission operation during normal driving.
- Similar deceptive practices have occurred before, with General Motors in the 1990s and Volkswagen itself in the 1970s, indicating a recurring issue in the automotive industry.
- Legal consequences for greenwashing include fines (up to $53,088 per violation by the FTC), cease-and-desist orders, and potential criminal prosecution, but enforcement is challenged by vague definitions of green practices and a lack of comprehensive federal CSR or ESG disclosure mandates in the U.S.
- When greenwashing is exposed, customers reassess the company’s intentions, often leading to loss of trust, negative attitudes, and decreased loyalty, as seen with VW’s leadership changes and public apologies after the scandal.
- Attribution theory plays a significant role in how stakeholders interpret CSR actions; if actions are perceived as insincere or manipulative, negative perceptions and behaviors follow, but genuine CSR efforts can still enhance a company’s reputation and stakeholder relationships.

## Common Shortcuts in Judging Others

- **Selective perception** means you unconsciously focus on information that aligns with your background, motivations, and characteristics, which can lead you to ignore other important data. For example, leaders may focus on financial results and overlook employee satisfaction unless specifically directed to value it.
- **Halo and horns effects** occur when you let a single positive trait (halo) or negative trait (horns) shape your overall impression of someone, even if unrelated to their actual abilities. For instance, assuming a friendly applicant is also competent (halo) or that a loud applicant is not (horns).
- **Contrast effects** happen when you evaluate someone not in isolation but in comparison to others you have recently encountered, which can distort your judgment—such as rating employees lower after seeing a star performer.
- **Stereotyping** involves judging individuals based on generalized beliefs about the group they belong to, which can lead to unfair treatment in processes like hiring and performance evaluation. Research shows that people with stereotypically white male names receive more positive responses from professors, and women (especially those with accents) are often perceived as less competent and warm.
- These perceptual shortcuts and biases can significantly distort your outlook and impact important organizational decisions, such as interviews, hiring, and promotions, often disadvantaging certain groups (e.g., women in the Indian police force).

## Specific Applications of Shortcuts in Organizations

- **Perceptual judgments in organizations are common and impactful**, influencing hiring, performance appraisals, and team dynamics. These judgments are often made quickly and can be inaccurate, especially during employment interviews where first impressions dominate.
- **Self-fulfilling prophecy shapes behavior**, meaning that people tend to act in ways that confirm others’ expectations. High expectations from managers can lead to better employee performance, while low expectations can result in poorer outcomes.
- **Social media amplifies shortcut evaluations**, allowing both employers and clients to make judgments based on limited or superficial information. This can lead to biased hiring decisions and significant organizational consequences, such as changes in stock value due to online reviews.
- **Use of social media in hiring is widespread but risky**, with about 70% of organizations screening candidates this way. This practice can introduce bias and legal risks, especially if it leads to discrimination against protected groups. Some states have enacted laws to protect applicant privacy.
- **Technology and AI can reduce or amplify bias**, depending on their design and use. Mechanical, data-driven methods are generally more effective than subjective ones, but AI systems can also introduce new biases, especially if they are trained on biased data. Human decision-makers may unconsciously adopt these biases.
- **Critical decisions should avoid overreliance on AI**, particularly when they affect significant resources or human welfare, due to the risk of perpetuating or amplifying bias.
- **Attention and memory lapses contribute to decision errors**, so organizations should foster environments that minimize distractions and support employee focus.
- **Stereotypes are dangerous cognitive shortcuts**, as they are based on generalizations that may not apply to individuals. It is important to self-monitor and avoid unfairly applying stereotypes in evaluations and decisions.

## Myth or Science?

- **Speed in decision making is often associated with progress and innovation, but can also lead to negative consequences if not managed carefully, such as neglecting quality or failing to involve key stakeholders.**
- **Including stakeholders who are most affected by decisions improves the quality of those decisions, as supported by research.**
- **Developing effective decision-making processes may take time initially, but ultimately enables organizations to implement changes more quickly and smoothly, as illustrated by the Navy Seals' adage, "slow is smooth, and smooth is fast."**
- **Examples like Satya Nadella at Microsoft show that making decisions with urgency can revitalize organizations, leading to both faster and more strategic actions.**
- **Research demonstrates a strong correlation between the speed and quality of decision making and overall organizational performance; organizations that make decisions quickly are twice as likely to make high-quality decisions compared to slower ones.**
- **Speed and quality in decision making are interdependent, and effective leaders balance urgency with a genuine concern for the well-being of customers, employees, and shareholders.**

## The Link Between Perception and Individual Decision Making

- **Perception significantly influences decision making**, as your interpretation of information shapes how you identify problems and choose among alternatives.
- **The volume of available data can overwhelm you**, leading to decision avoidance or "decision distress" (regret, guilt, or doubt after making a decision), rather than improving decision quality.
- **Decision making is triggered by recognizing a problem**, which is a gap between the current state and a desired state, and your perception determines whether you notice and how you define this gap.
- **You must interpret and evaluate information from multiple sources**, deciding what is relevant based on your perceptions, which can be accurate or biased.
- **Context and motivation affect perception and decision making**; for example, during economic instability, employees may ignore organizational problems if they feel insecure about job opportunities, showing how perceptions can lead to denial or avoidance of issues.

## Decision Making in Organizations

- **Rational decision-making models are commonly taught but do not always reflect real-world decision processes**, as people often deviate from purely logical approaches.
- **Organizational Behavior (OB) addresses both perceptual and decision-making errors**, helping you understand and improve how decisions are made within organizations.
- **Common decision-making approaches and frequent errors** are important to recognize, as these can lead to biases and mistakes in organizational contexts.

## The Rational Decision-Making Model, Bounded Rationality, and Intuition

- **The rational decision-making model** assumes you have complete information, can identify all relevant options without bias, and will choose the option with the highest utility, following a structured six-step process. However, in reality, most decisions do not follow this model due to practical constraints.
- **Bounded rationality** recognizes that your cognitive limitations and the complexity of real-world problems prevent fully rational decisions. Instead, you simplify problems, focus on essential features, and seek solutions that are "good enough" (satisficing) rather than optimal. Satisficing can be effective and adaptive, as people dynamically adjust their planning based on the situation and their mental resources.
- **Bounded rationality impacts ethical decision making** because your perceptions of what is right or permissible are influenced by your duties to others and cognitive shortcuts. To improve decisions, you should ask multiple questions, use diverse information sources, evaluate those sources, and allow enough time for deliberation.
- **Intuitive decision making** is based on instinctive, unconscious processes shaped by experience and emotion. It is fast and holistic but not always rational or reliable. Intuition can be useful in urgent or uncertain situations, but it should be tested against objective data and rational analysis to avoid errors and biases.
- **Objective decision-making processes** help correct for perceptual and cognitive biases, but all approaches—rational, bounded rationality, and intuition—have their own strengths and limitations depending on the context.

## Common Biases and Errors in Decision Making

- **Systematic biases and errors often affect decision making** because people rely on experience, impulses, gut feelings, and convenient rules of thumb rather than rational analysis.
- **Strategies to reduce biases include**: setting clear goals to focus attention, seeking disconfirming evidence to challenge assumptions, accepting randomness to avoid false pattern recognition, and increasing options to improve decision quality.
- **Overconfidence bias** is the tendency to overestimate your own or others’ abilities, often without awareness. It can influence hiring and leadership selection, sometimes leading to initial success but eventual reputational damage when overconfidence is revealed.
- **Anchoring bias** occurs when you fixate on initial information (the "anchor") and fail to adjust adequately to new information. This is common in negotiations and decision-making, where the first number or idea presented heavily influences subsequent judgments.
- **Confirmation bias** leads you to seek out and accept information that supports your existing beliefs and choices, while discounting contradictory evidence. This affects how you perceive people and situations, reinforcing initial impressions.
- **Availability bias** is the tendency to base judgments on information that is most readily available or recent, especially if it is vivid or emotional. This can distort risk assessments and influence decisions in high-pressure environments, such as emergency rooms or performance appraisals.
- **Escalation of commitment (sunk cost effect)** is the tendency to persist with a failing course of action due to prior investments, responsibility, or hope of eventual success, even when evidence suggests you should change direction. Sharing decision authority and collective emotions can increase escalation, but de-escalation is viewed as more trustworthy.
- **Randomness error** is the belief that you can predict or control random events, leading to superstitions or seeing patterns where none exist.
- **Risk aversion** is the preference for a sure thing over a risky outcome, even when the risky option has a higher expected value. Decision framing (gain vs. loss), job security, and gender diversity in leadership can influence risk aversion.
- **Hindsight bias** is the tendency to believe, after an outcome is known, that you would have predicted it accurately, making events seem more predictable than they were.
- **Outcome bias** is judging the quality of a decision based on the outcome rather than the decision-making process itself, which can lead to overlooking flawed methods if the result is positive.
- **Reducing bias involves** reflecting and reframing problems, using structured decision-making approaches, and setting explicit standards to focus on alternatives and reduce reliance on salient but potentially misleading information.

## Influences on Decision Making: Individual Differences and Organizational Constraints

- **Individual differences such as personality, experience, and cognitive style impact how you make decisions and your susceptibility to biases.**
- **Organizational constraints, including rules, policies, and resource limitations, shape your decision-making process and can increase or decrease the likelihood of errors and biases.**
- **The degree to which you are affected by decision-making biases varies based on these personal and organizational factors, meaning not everyone is equally vulnerable.**

## Individual Differences

- **Personality traits significantly influence decision-making strategies and susceptibility to biases.** For example, individuals who trust their intuition may make riskier decisions or judge unethical behavior more harshly. High self-esteem leads to self-serving biases, such as blaming others for failures and taking credit for successes. Narcissists are prone to overconfidence and self-serving biases.
- **Big Five personality traits impact decision making.** Higher openness reduces selective perception and confirmation bias, as open individuals are more receptive to contradictory information. Extraversion increases confidence in decisions but not accuracy, and extraverted CEOs are more likely to take risks, such as pursuing acquisitions.
- **Gender identity shows minimal influence on decision making.** Most research supports the gender similarities hypothesis, indicating little to no psychological differences between genders. Where differences exist, such as men being more prone to sensation seeking and risk taking, the overlap between genders is substantial and differences often diminish with age.
- **Intellectual abilities affect decision making, but not immunity to errors.** Higher general mental ability (GMA) allows for faster information processing and more accurate problem solving, but does not prevent all decision errors, especially those related to overconfidence or emotional defensiveness. Cognitive flexibility, the ability to adapt decision strategies to the problem, leads to better decisions in both structured and unstructured situations.
- **Cultural background shapes decision-making processes.** Culture influences problem selection, analysis depth, the value placed on logic, and whether decisions are made individually or collectively. For example, North American culture values overt rationality, Japanese culture emphasizes consensus and cooperation, and Confucian values in China promote ethical decision making.

## Organizational Constraints

- **Organizational constraints can cause deviations from rational decision-making** by influencing managers through performance evaluation systems, reward systems, formal regulations, time constraints, and historical precedents.
- **Performance evaluation systems shape managerial decisions** by encouraging behaviors that align with evaluation criteria, sometimes leading to information distortion and suboptimal choices if managers prioritize appearance or avoidance of negative feedback over effectiveness.
- **Reward systems impact decision making** by signaling which actions yield better personal outcomes; aggressive or unrealistic incentives can drive dysfunctional or unethical behaviors, as seen in the Wells Fargo example where employees created fake accounts to meet targets.
- **Formal regulations and company policies limit decision choices** but also help structure the decision-making process to reduce bias and ensure consistency, such as in CEO succession planning, which improves the quality and quantity of candidates.
- **Time constraints force managers to make decisions under deadlines**, often without complete information, which can negatively affect the quality of decisions.
- **Historical precedents influence current decisions**, as past choices set a context and framework for present actions, such as budget allocations being heavily influenced by previous years’ budgets.
- **Employee autonomy is often limited by organizational rules and standards**, ensuring consistency in quality, safety, and service, as illustrated by food preparation protocols at Five Guys.

## Ethics in Decision Making

- **Ethical decision making can be approached using three main criteria:** the utilitarian approach (focusing on outcomes and the greatest good for the greatest number), the rights approach (emphasizing respect for individual rights and liberties), and the justice approach (prioritizing fairness and equitable treatment).
- **Lying negatively impacts decision making** by undermining trust, distorting information, and potentially leading to unethical outcomes regardless of the ethical framework applied.
- **The choice of ethical criterion influences organizational decisions** by shaping how you evaluate actions, balance competing interests, and justify outcomes.

## Three Ethical Decision Criteria

- **Utilitarianism focuses on making decisions based on outcomes, aiming to achieve the greatest good for the greatest number.** This approach aligns with business goals like rationality, efficiency, productivity, and profit, but its application can be influenced by cultural values and emotional distance, as shown in studies where responses to moral dilemmas varied by country and language context.
- **A rights-based ethical criterion emphasizes making decisions that respect and protect fundamental liberties and privileges, such as privacy, free speech, and due process.** This approach safeguards individuals, including whistleblowers, by upholding their basic rights even when exposing unethical practices.
- **Justice as an ethical criterion involves applying rules and distributing benefits and costs fairly and impartially.** This is often rooted in deontological (duty-based) ethics, where individuals feel obligated to act according to moral norms, principles, or laws, regardless of organizational rules, and perceive actions as unjust if they violate these standards.

## Choosing Among Criteria

- **Utilitarianism is commonly used in for-profit organizations** because it focuses on actions that maximize overall benefits, often justifying decisions like layoffs, price increases, or moving production overseas to improve efficiency and profits.
- **There is no single, universally accepted ethical standard** for evaluating decisions, making it challenging to balance utilitarian outcomes with individual rights and social justice, which introduce more ambiguity into ethical decision-making.
- **Ethical decision-making is complex and evolving**, with a shift from viewing utilitarianism, rights, and justice as separate criteria to recognizing their interrelatedness, especially depending on whether decisions affect oneself or others.
- **Corporate social responsibility (CSR) addresses these complexities** by encouraging organizations to consider the broader impact of their actions, not just financial outcomes.
- **CSR initiatives can positively affect organizational performance** by attracting consumers who prefer responsible companies, drawing high-performing employees, gaining government incentives for sustainability, and ultimately benefiting the organization's bottom line.

## Behavioral Ethics

- **Behavioral ethics examines why individuals sometimes act unethically, even when they are aware of ethical standards.**
- Ethical behavior is influenced by both collective standards (societal and organizational) and personal ethics, but people do not always adhere to these standards.
- Research shows that factors such as employee performance can affect ethical judgments; for instance, supervisors may be less punitive toward unethical actions by high-performing employees.
- **Ethical behavior is highly situational and can vary significantly depending on the context.**
- **Culture plays a significant role in shaping ethical decision-making, and there are few universal ethical standards.**
- Actions considered ethical in one culture may be viewed as unethical in another, such as the acceptability of bribery in some countries versus others.
- Organizations that lack awareness of cultural differences in ethics may inadvertently promote unethical behavior.

## Lying

- **Lying and dishonest behavior are very common**, influenced by both situational factors and individual differences.
- **Detecting lies is difficult**; people correctly identify liars only 47% of the time, which is worse than random chance.
- **Lying undermines decision making** because misrepresented facts and false motives prevent managers and employees from making sound choices.
- **Lying presents a significant ethical issue** within organizations.
- **Lie-detection techniques and entrapment are unreliable** for identifying dishonesty in organizational settings.
- **The most effective approach is to prevent lying** by creating environments that discourage dishonest behavior, aligning with principles from organizational behavior (OB).

## An Ethical Choice

- **Corporate scandals often highlight large-scale ethical violations, but everyday unethical behaviors—such as minor expense report alterations, office supply theft, and conflicts of interest—are widespread and frequently overlooked.**
- **Frequent minor unethical acts can create negative organizational norms and erode trust, and if unchecked, these small violations can escalate into more serious offenses.**
- **Behavioral ethics research shows that over half of people engage in unethical behavior, and about one in four U.S. employees have witnessed workplace misconduct; many do so without malicious intent due to "blind spots"—a lack of awareness that their actions are unethical.**
- **Unethical behavior is more likely when behavioral norms are unclear, victims are not visible, or performance goals are unrealistic, as seen in the Boeing example.**
- **Recommendations to prevent unethical behavior include:**
    - Raising awareness and clarifying what constitutes unethical and illegal behavior through training and education.
    - Clearly communicating the consequences of misconduct, especially when actions are deliberate and harmful.
    - Encouraging employees to report unethical practices by establishing safe and effective whistleblowing channels and removing barriers like fear of retaliation.
    - Implementing both formal systems (such as hiring, training, performance management, and accountability structures) and informal systems (like ethical role models, norms, rituals, and language) to detect, monitor, and prevent everyday unethical behavior.

## Creativity, Creative Decision Making, and Innovation in Organizations

- **Creativity in organizations involves generating novel and useful ideas** that are different from previous solutions but still appropriate for the problem.
- **The three-stage model of creativity includes:** causes (predictors of creative behavior), creative behavior itself, and effects (outcomes of creative behavior).
- **Causes (predictors) of creative behavior** help explain why and when individuals are likely to be creative, while **effects (outcomes)** describe the impact and results of creative actions within the organization.
- **Creative behavior is central to the model**, serving as the link between what influences creativity and the results it produces in organizational settings.

## Creative Behavior

- **Creative behavior follows four key steps:** problem formulation, information gathering, idea generation, and idea evaluation, each building on the previous stage.
- **Problem formulation** involves identifying a problem or opportunity that needs a novel solution, as seen in the development of the UK’s first cloud-based AI radiology device, which began with recognizing hospital inefficiencies.
- **Information gathering** is the process of seeking knowledge and allowing ideas to incubate, which helps uncover innovation opportunities; maintaining curiosity and avoiding routine is crucial to prevent stagnation.
- **Idea generation** is driven by curiosity and involves developing possible solutions from gathered information, often benefiting from collaborative efforts but also facing challenges like coordination costs and competition for credit.
- **Idea evaluation** requires selecting the best solution from those generated, ideally by individuals different from the idea creators to avoid ideator’s bias and diffusion of responsibility; hierarchy can aid this phase by clarifying decision-making authority.
- **Group dynamics impact creativity:** Exposure to others’ opinions before forming your own can lead to conformity and reduced creativity, as shown in studies where independent forecasting led to more accurate group outcomes than when predictions were shared beforehand.
- **Preserving independent thinking** throughout both idea generation and evaluation is essential to avoid groupthink and ensure diverse, high-quality creative outcomes.

## Causes of Creative Behavior

- **Creative potential is influenced by intelligence, personality, expertise, and ethics.** Higher intelligence, especially working memory and a desire to learn, supports creativity. Personality traits such as openness to experience, proactivity, self-confidence, risk-taking, tolerance for ambiguity, perseverance, hope, self-efficacy, and positive affect are linked to greater creativity. Expertise in a field, as well as access to diverse social networks, enhances creative potential. The relationship between creativity and ethics is complex; creativity may sometimes be associated with rule-breaking, but creative individuals can also be more imaginative about moral consequences and thus more ethical.
    
- **A supportive creative environment is essential for realizing creative potential.** Intrinsic motivation—working on tasks because they are interesting, exciting, satisfying, or challenging—strongly correlates with creative outcomes. Environments that reward and recognize creative work, encourage the free flow of ideas, and provide fair and constructive judgment foster creativity. Freedom from excessive rules and autonomy in deciding how work is done also support creative behavior.
    
- **Job characteristics and managerial support play a significant role in fostering creativity.** Jobs that are complex, autonomous, and have clear expectations for innovation encourage creative behavior. Managers can enhance creativity by helping employees find resources for their ideas, providing appropriate tools, and encouraging knowledge sharing. Linking teams to broader networks and resources, especially through weaker ties outside immediate team networks, increases the likelihood of radical, status-quo-breaking creativity.
    

## Creative Outcomes (Innovation)

- **Creative behavior does not always lead to innovation**—ideas may not be shared, may be rejected by management, or suppressed by teams, and leadership style can negatively impact organizational innovation.
- **Innovation is defined as ideas or solutions that are both novel and useful** to relevant stakeholders; novelty alone is not sufficient—usefulness is essential for an idea to be considered innovative.
- **Acceptance of creative ideas can be hindered by uncertainty**, as people may resist new ideas that disrupt the status quo.
- **Family-owned businesses are often more successful at turning creative ideas into innovations** due to strong relational ties, as shown by studies across multiple countries.
- **Translating creative ideas into innovative outcomes is a social process** that depends on factors like power, politics, leadership, and motivation within the organization.
