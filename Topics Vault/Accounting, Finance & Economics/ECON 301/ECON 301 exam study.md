## How Professor Zou Tests This Material

Before diving into content, here is a breakdown of the question types you should expect, based on the practice exam pattern.

### Type 1: Definitional and Conceptual Multiple Choice

These test whether you know a term cold, no math required. Examples of the style: identifying whether a scenario is consumer to consumer rivalry vs producer to producer rivalry vs producer to consumer rivalry, classifying a good as private, common, club, or public, identifying whether a good is normal or inferior, or picking the correct definition of accounting profit vs economic profit vs opportunity cost.

**Strategy:** Make sure you can define every bolded term in these notes in one sentence, and that you can tell the difference between closely related pairs (accounting profit vs economic profit, price ceiling vs price floor, binding vs non-binding, excise tax vs ad valorem tax).

### Type 2: Graph Reading Questions

You will be shown a supply and demand diagram (or asked to picture one) and asked to identify an area, such as deadweight loss (DWL) from a price ceiling, price floor, or tax. These ask you to identify which triangle or rectangle represents consumer surplus, producer surplus, tax revenue, or DWL.

**Strategy:** Practice sketching supply and demand by hand. Label equilibrium price and quantity, then shade CS above price and below demand, PS below price and above supply. When a price control or tax is added, get comfortable identifying the new triangle of lost surplus (DWL) versus who captures the remaining surplus.

### Type 3: Calculation Questions

This is the biggest category. These give you numbers and ask you to solve for a value. Common setups seen on the practice exam include: computing NPV of a project, computing present value of a single future payment, computing quantity supplied or demanded from a linear demand or supply function, solving for market equilibrium price and quantity, calculating consumer or producer surplus (triangle area), and calculating an elasticity value from a demand function or from percentage changes.

**Strategy:** Practice these formulas until the algebra is automatic:

- Setting Qd = Qs to solve for equilibrium
- Plugging values into a linear demand or supply function
- Triangle area formula: 0.5 times base times height
- Elasticity formula: (dQ/dP) times (P/Q)

### Type 4: Formula Application and Derivation Questions

These ask you to take a demand or supply function like Qx = 100 minus 3Px plus 4Py minus 0.01M and either compute a value, find the inverse function, or identify the sign and meaning of a coefficient (is this good a substitute or complement, normal or inferior, and why).

**Strategy:** Know how to flip a demand function from Q in terms of P into P in terms of Q (the inverse demand function), since this is what you graph. Know that the coefficient sign tells you the relationship (positive cross price coefficient means substitutes, negative means complements; positive income coefficient means normal good, negative means inferior good).

### Type 5: Tax Incidence and Policy Application

Questions describing a tax, price ceiling, or price floor being introduced, and asking what happens to price, quantity, shortage, surplus, or DWL. These often combine a graph reading step with a calculation step.

**Strategy:** Memorize the four outcomes table for simultaneous demand and supply shifts (see Lecture 2 section below), and know that a binding price ceiling always causes a shortage while a binding price floor always causes a surplus.

## Lecture 1: Fundamentals

### The Seven Principles of Effective Managerial Decision Making

Professor Zou's framework for the course. These recur throughout every lecture:

1. Identify goals and constraints
2. Recognize the nature and importance of profits (accounting vs economic)
3. Understand incentives
4. Understand markets (Five Forces)
5. Recognize the time value of money
6. Use marginal analysis
7. Make data driven decisions

### Principle 2: Profits

- **Accounting profit:** total revenue minus explicit (out of pocket) costs.
- **Economic profit:** total revenue minus explicit costs minus implicit costs (opportunity costs).
- **Opportunity cost:** the value of the next best alternative you gave up to do what you are doing. Includes the opportunity cost of your own time and capital, not just cash outlays.
- **Sunk cost:** a cost that has already been incurred and cannot be recovered. Sunk costs should never affect a forward looking decision (this is the sunk cost fallacy: continuing to invest in something just because you already spent money on it).

**Worked example logic (Maria's bakery, seen on practice exam style):** If Maria gives up a $60,000 salary to open a bakery, that $60,000 is an implicit cost (opportunity cost of her labor). If the bakery brings in $150,000 revenue and pays $100,000 in explicit costs, accounting profit is $50,000, but economic profit is $50,000 minus the $60,000 opportunity cost, which equals negative $10,000.

### Principle 3: Incentives

People and firms respond to incentives, but incentives can backfire (perverse incentives). Key behavioral studies covered:

- **Gneezy and Rustichini (daycare fines):** Daycares started fining parents for picking up kids late. Late pickups increased, because the fine reframed lateness from a moral violation into a paid service, crowding out the intrinsic motivation to be on time.
- **Haifa IQ test study:** Paying students for performance on an IQ style test improved effort and scores, showing that in some settings extrinsic financial incentives do work.
- **Imas (self vs charity effort):** People exert less effort when working for charity once given the option to quit partway through, compared to working for themselves, suggesting that the type of incentive and how commitment is framed matters.

**Takeaway:** Incentives are powerful but the design matters. A poorly designed incentive can crowd out intrinsic motivation and make the targeted behavior worse (perverse incentive).

### Principle 4: Understand Markets, the Five Forces Framework

A framework for analyzing the competitive environment of an industry:

1. **Entry:** how easy is it for new firms to enter this market
2. **Power of suppliers:** how much bargaining power do input suppliers have
3. **Power of buyers:** how much bargaining power do customers have
4. **Industry rivalry:** how intense is competition among existing firms
5. **Substitutes and complements:** availability of substitute goods, and the role of complementary goods

### Market Rivalries

Three types of rivalry exist in a market:

- **Consumer vs consumer rivalry:** buyers compete against each other for a limited supply of a good (bidding up price).
- **Consumer vs producer rivalry:** buyers and sellers negotiate over price, each wanting a better deal for themselves.
- **Producer vs producer rivalry:** sellers compete against each other for customers.

### Principle 5: Time Value of Money

People prefer money today over the same amount of money in the future. Reasons: impatience, uncertainty about the future, and option value (money today can be invested for more opportunities).

**Future Value (FV):** what a sum today will be worth in the future given compounding interest. FV = (1 + i)^n times PV where i is the interest rate and n is the number of periods.

**Present Value (PV):** what a future sum is worth today. PV = FV / (1 + i)^n

**Present value of a stream of future payments:** PV = FV1/(1+i)^1 + FV2/(1+i)^2 + FV3/(1+i)^3 + ... + FVn/(1+i)^n

**Net Present Value (NPV):** the present value of an investment's benefits minus the present value of its costs (or minus the initial cost C0). NPV = [sum of discounted future values] minus C0

Decision rule:

- If NPV is greater than 0, the deal is good (benefits outweigh costs). Accept.
- If NPV is less than 0, the deal is bad (costs outweigh benefits). Reject.
- If NPV equals 0, you are indifferent.

**Perpetuity formula (an asset or payment stream that continues forever with the same cash flow CF each period):** PV of perpetuity = CF / i

**Present value of a firm with constant profit growth rate g (where g is less than i), starting from current profit pi-zero:** PV of firm = pi-zero times [(1 + i) / (i minus g)]

**Worked example logic:** If your uncle borrows $100 and promises to pay back $150 in five years, and you could otherwise earn 10 percent interest, the present value of his offer is 150/(1.1)^5 = about $93.14. Since you gave up $100 to receive something worth only $93.14 today, NPV = 93.14 minus 100 = negative $6.86. Bad deal.

**Exam tip:** These are calculation heavy. Practice discounting a single payment, discounting a stream of uneven payments across different years, and using the perpetuity shortcut. Watch for problems where costs and benefits happen in different years (you must discount each one back to today individually before subtracting).

### Principle 6: Marginal Analysis

- **Total benefit** B(Q) and **total cost** C(Q) as functions of a control variable Q.
- **Net benefit:** N(Q) = B(Q) minus C(Q). The manager's objective is to maximize this.
- **Marginal benefit MB(Q):** the change in total benefit from a one unit change in Q (the slope of the total benefit curve, or the derivative).
- **Marginal cost MC(Q):** the change in total cost from a one unit change in Q (the slope of the total cost curve).
- **Marginal net benefit MNB(Q):** MB(Q) minus MC(Q).

**The golden rule:** net benefits are maximized where MB(Q) = MC(Q), which is the same point where MNB(Q) = 0. If MB is greater than MC, keep increasing Q (you are leaving money on the table). If MB is less than MC, decrease Q.

**Worked example logic:** If B(Q) = 250Q minus 4Q^2 and C(Q) = Q^2, then MB(Q) = 250 minus 8Q and MC(Q) = 2Q. Setting them equal: 250 minus 8Q = 2Q, so 250 = 10Q, so Q = 25.

**"Thumbs up or thumbs down" decisions (yes or no decisions, not "how many"):**

- Incremental revenue: the additional revenue from a yes decision
- Incremental cost: the additional cost from a yes decision
- Say yes if MB is greater than MC (incremental revenue exceeds incremental cost). Say no otherwise.

### Principle 7: Make Data Driven Decisions

Managers use regression analysis to estimate demand functions from real world data.

**Simple regression model:** Y = a + bX + e, where a is the unknown population intercept, b is the unknown population slope, and e is a random error term.

**Least squares (OLS) regression line:** Y-hat = a-hat + b-hat times X. The estimates a-hat and b-hat are chosen to minimize the sum of squared errors between the line and the actual data points.

**Multiple regression:** extends this to several explanatory variables at once, Y = a + b1X1 + b2X2 + ... + bkXk + e. This lets you estimate a full demand function (quantity as a function of price, income, price of related goods, and other factors) from data all at once.

**Log-linear regression:** taking the natural log of both sides, ln(Y) = a + b times ln(X), transforms the equation so that b-hat is directly interpretable as an elasticity (a percentage change relationship). This connects directly to Lecture 4.

**Correlation is not causation:** Running a regression and finding a relationship between two variables (for example, wine consumption and lifespan) does not prove that one causes the other.

- **Omitted variable bias:** a third variable (like wealth, or climate) could be driving both variables you are studying.
- **Selection effects:** people are not randomly assigned to a behavior. People who choose to drink wine, or who choose to join a job training program, may differ in unobservable ways (risk tolerance, motivation) from those who do not, and that difference, not the behavior itself, may explain the outcome.
- **Randomization and experiments:** the gold standard fix. Randomly assigning people to a treatment group and a control group (for example, flipping a coin to decide who gets a job training program) ensures that both observed and unobserved traits are, on average, balanced between groups, so any difference in outcomes can be attributed to the treatment itself.

## Lecture 2: Market Forces

### The Law of Demand and Law of Supply

- **Law of demand:** holding all else constant, as the price of a good rises, the quantity demanded of that good falls (inverse relationship between P and Qd).
- **Law of supply:** holding all else constant, as the price of a good rises, the quantity supplied of that good rises (direct relationship between P and Qs).

### Demand Shifters (non price factors that shift the entire demand curve)

Income, prices of related goods (substitutes and complements), tastes and preferences, number of buyers, expectations about future prices.

### Supply Shifters (non price factors that shift the entire supply curve)

Input prices, technology, prices of related goods in production, number of sellers, expectations, taxes and subsidies.

### The Linear Demand Function

Qx-d = beta-zero + beta-x times Px + beta-r times Pr + beta-M times M + beta-H times H

Where Qx-d is quantity demanded of good X, Px is the price of good X, Pr is the price of a related good, M is income, and H is any other demand shifting variable.

**Reading the coefficient signs:**

- beta-x is negative (law of demand: as own price rises, quantity demanded falls)
- If beta-r is positive, goods X and R are substitutes (a price increase in R increases demand for X)
- If beta-r is negative, goods X and R are complements (a price increase in R decreases demand for X)
- If beta-M is positive, X is a normal good (as income rises, demand rises)
- If beta-M is negative, X is an inferior good (as income rises, demand falls)

### The Linear Supply Function

Qx-s = beta-zero + beta-x times Px + beta-r times Pr + beta-w times W + beta-H times H

Where W is the price of an input. By the law of supply, beta-x is positive. beta-w is typically negative (higher input costs reduce the quantity firms are willing to supply at a given price). beta-r positive would mean technology or a related good's price lowers the cost of producing X.

### Inverse Demand and Inverse Supply Functions

The inverse function solves the linear function for P in terms of Q instead of Q in terms of P. This is the form you actually graph (P on the vertical axis).

**Worked example logic:** If Qx-s = 2,000 + 3Px minus 4Pt minus 1Pw, and you plug in Pt = $250 and Pw = $1,400, this simplifies to Qx-s = 3Px minus 400. Solving for Px gives the inverse supply function: Px = 400/3 + (1/3) times Qx-s.

### Consumer Surplus and Producer Surplus

- **Consumer surplus (CS):** the amount consumers benefit from paying a price lower than the maximum they were willing to pay (willingness to pay, or WTP). Geometrically, the triangle area above the equilibrium price and below the demand curve.
- **Producer surplus (PS):** the amount producers benefit from receiving a price higher than the minimum they were willing to accept (willingness to accept, or WTA). Geometrically, the triangle area below the equilibrium price and above the supply curve.

**Triangle area formula:** Surplus = 0.5 times base times height

**Worked example logic (producer surplus):** If a seller's supply curve is P = 44 + 2Q, then at Q = 20 the marginal cost of that 20th unit is 44 + 2(20) = $84. If the market price is $100, that seller earns 100 minus 84 = $16 of producer surplus on that unit.

### Market Equilibrium

- **Competitive market equilibrium:** the price and quantity where Qd = Qs, meaning there is no shortage or surplus and no pressure for price to change.
- Solve by setting the demand and supply equations equal to each other and solving for P, then plug back in to get Q.
- If Qd is greater than Qs at a given price, there is a **shortage**, and price will rise to clear it.
- If Qd is less than Qs at a given price, there is a **surplus**, and price will fall to clear it.

**Worked example logic:** Qd = 10 minus 2P and Qs = 2 + 2P. Setting equal: 10 minus 2P = 2 + 2P, so 8 = 4P, so P equals $2, and plugging back in gives Q equals 6 units.

### Price Controls

- **Price ceiling:** a legal maximum price. Binding only if set below the equilibrium price, in which case it causes a **shortage** (Qd exceeds Qs at the ceiling price).
- **Price floor:** a legal minimum price. Binding only if set above the equilibrium price, in which case it causes a **surplus** (Qs exceeds Qd at the floor price).
- **Non-binding** price controls (a ceiling above equilibrium, or a floor below equilibrium) have no effect on the market outcome.

**Deadweight loss (DWL) from a price ceiling:** the triangle of lost total surplus that occurs because the quantity traded falls from the efficient quantity Qe down to the ceiling-constrained quantity Qs. This lost surplus is never captured by anyone.

**Full economic price under a ceiling:** since ceilings often create shortages and non-price rationing (like long lines), the true cost to buyers, called the full economic price PF, equals the dollar price paid Pc plus the nonpecuniary cost of waiting (PF minus Pc). PF = Pc + (PF minus Pc)

**Price floor nuances:** the cost of a price floor depends on how it is implemented.

- **Minimum wage style floor:** firms simply cannot pay less than the floor. The DWL is only the standard triangle.
- **Agricultural price support style floor:** if sellers are allowed to produce and sell as much as they want at the floor price, they will overproduce relative to what is demanded, and those extra unsold units represent wasted production cost (an additional loss beyond the standard triangle). If the government buys up the surplus, the total cost to the government is PF times (Qs minus Qd), and this can still be deadweight loss if the government cannot resell or redistribute the surplus product.

### Taxes

Taxes shift the supply curve up and to the left (decreasing supply at any given price).

- **Excise tax:** a flat, per unit tax on each unit sold. Tax revenue is collected from the supplier per unit.
- **Ad valorem tax:** a percentage tax based on the value (price) of the good.

Both types raise the price consumers pay and lower the price sellers effectively keep, and both reduce the quantity traded below the free market equilibrium, creating deadweight loss.

### Comparative Statics

**Comparative static analysis** studies the movement from one market equilibrium to another after a shock.

**Single curve shifts:**

- Demand increases alone: equilibrium price and quantity both increase.
- Demand decreases alone: equilibrium price and quantity both decrease.
- Supply increases alone: equilibrium price decreases, equilibrium quantity increases.
- Supply decreases alone: equilibrium price increases, equilibrium quantity decreases.

**Simultaneous shifts (memorize this table, it is exam relevant):**

|Change|Increase in Demand|Decrease in Demand|
|---|---|---|
|Increase in Supply|Price: ambiguous. Quantity: increases|Price: decreases. Quantity: ambiguous|
|Decrease in Supply|Price: increases. Quantity: ambiguous|Price: ambiguous. Quantity: decreases|

**How to remember this:** whenever both curves push price (or quantity) in the same direction, that outcome is certain. Whenever they push in opposite directions, that outcome is ambiguous and depends on the relative size of the two shifts.

## Lecture 3: Market Failures

A market failure occurs when the free market does not lead to an efficient allocation of resources. Four main sources covered in this course: externalities, public goods, common resources (tragedy of the commons), and incomplete or asymmetric information.

### Externalities

An externality occurs when a third party who is not part of a transaction is affected by it, and that effect is not reflected in the market price.

- **Negative externality:** a cost imposed on a third party (example: a paper mill polluting a river that harms downstream residents). Left alone, the free market will **overproduce** relative to the socially efficient quantity, because the private cost of production is lower than the true social cost.
- **Positive externality:** a benefit conferred on a third party (example: vaccination reducing disease spread to others, or education raising civic engagement). Left alone, the free market will **underproduce** relative to the socially efficient quantity.

**Coase Theorem:** if property rights are clearly defined and transaction costs are low (parties can negotiate cheaply), private parties can bargain to resolve an externality efficiently on their own, regardless of who initially holds the property right.

### Public Goods and the Free Rider Problem

A public good is both **non-excludable** (you cannot stop people from using it) and **non-rival** (one person's use does not reduce availability to others).

Because people cannot be excluded from enjoying a public good once it exists, individuals have an incentive to **free ride**, letting others pay for it while they enjoy it for free. This causes private markets to **underprovide** public goods relative to the efficient level.

**Types of public goods:**

- **Linear public good:** total benefit keeps rising as more is funded (example: streetlights, more funding means more lights and more benefit).
- **Threshold public good:** either it gets built or it does not, based on hitting a funding threshold (example: a single bridge that needs $1 billion; below that, nothing gets built). These can have multiple equilibria: if nobody else is contributing, your best move is not to contribute either, but if everyone else is paying their equal share, your best move is to also pay your share to hit the threshold.

### The Four Types of Goods (by rivalry and excludability)

||Excludable|Non-Excludable|
|---|---|---|
|**Rival**|Private good (a sandwich)|Common resource (ocean fish)|
|**Non-Rival**|Club good (cable TV, a toll road with no congestion)|Public good (national defense, streetlights)|

### Tragedy of the Commons

Applies to **common resources** (rival but non-excludable, like open ocean fisheries or public grazing land). Because no one owns the resource and everyone can access it, individuals overuse it since they capture the full benefit of their own use but only bear a fraction of the cost of depletion. This leads to **overuse and depletion** relative to the efficient level, the mirror image of the free rider underprovision problem for public goods.

### Incomplete and Asymmetric Information

Efficiently functioning markets require that participants have reasonably good information about prices, quality, available technology, and risk. When information is incomplete, markets fail to reach efficient outcomes.

**Asymmetric information:** occurs when one side of a transaction has better information than the other.

**Akerlof's "Market for Lemons" (1970), the classic example, using the used car market:**

1. Sellers know whether their car is a lemon (bad quality) or good quality; buyers do not know which is which.
2. Buyers, unable to tell quality apart, will only pay an average price reflecting the average quality in the market.
3. This average price is a good deal for lemon sellers but a bad deal for quality car sellers.
4. Quality car sellers start leaving the market rather than accept the low average price.
5. As quality sellers exit, the average quality (and the average price buyers are willing to pay) falls further.
6. This unravels until only lemons remain in the market. This phenomenon is called **adverse selection**.

**Government responses to incomplete information problems:** rules against insider trading, certification requirements, truth in lending laws, truth in advertising laws, and enforcement of contracts.

## Lecture 4: Elasticity

### Own Price Elasticity of Demand

Measures how responsive quantity demanded is to a change in the good's own price.

Formula: Own price elasticity (E) = (dQ/dP) times (P/Q) = percent change in quantity demanded divided by percent change in price

**Interpretation categories (use absolute value):**

- |E| greater than 1: demand is **elastic** (quantity is very responsive to price)
- |E| less than 1: demand is **inelastic** (quantity is not very responsive to price)
- |E| equal to 1: demand is **unitary elastic**
- |E| equal to 0: demand is **perfectly inelastic** (vertical demand curve)
- |E| equal to infinity: demand is **perfectly elastic** (horizontal demand curve)

Own price elasticity of demand is always negative or zero by the law of demand (as price rises, quantity falls), so professors often refer to its absolute value when discussing magnitude.

### Determinants of Elasticity

Demand tends to be more elastic when: there are many close substitutes available, the good is a larger share of the consumer's budget, the time horizon is longer (more time to adjust behavior), and the good is a luxury rather than a necessity.

### Elasticity Along a Linear Demand Curve

On a straight line demand curve, elasticity is **not constant**. It changes at every point along the curve:

- Near the top of the demand curve (high price, low quantity): demand is elastic
- Near the bottom of the demand curve (low price, high quantity): demand is inelastic
- At the exact midpoint of the demand curve: demand is unitary elastic

### The Relationship Between Elasticity and Total Revenue

This is one of the most tested concepts in the elasticity unit.

- If demand is **elastic**, raising price **decreases** total revenue (quantity falls proportionally more than price rises), and lowering price **increases** total revenue.
- If demand is **inelastic**, raising price **increases** total revenue (quantity falls proportionally less than price rises), and lowering price **decreases** total revenue.
- If demand is **unitary elastic**, total revenue is at its maximum, and small price changes do not change total revenue.

**Marginal revenue (MR) and elasticity:** MR is derived from total revenue TR = P times Q using the product rule from calculus. The resulting relationship shows that MR is positive when demand is elastic, MR is zero when demand is unitary elastic, and MR is negative when demand is inelastic. This is why a profit maximizing firm with market power will never intentionally operate in the inelastic portion of its demand curve.

### Cross Price Elasticity of Demand

Measures how responsive quantity demanded of good X is to a change in the price of a different good Y.

Formula: Cross price elasticity = (dQx/dPy) times (Py/Qx)

**Interpretation:**

- Positive cross price elasticity: the two goods are **substitutes** (a price increase in Y increases demand for X)
- Negative cross price elasticity: the two goods are **complements** (a price increase in Y decreases demand for X)

**Worked example logic:** If demand is given by Qd = negative 2Px minus 0.5Py plus 3M, and at the relevant point Qx = 4, Py = 4, then cross price elasticity = negative 0.5 times (4/4) = negative 0.5. Since this is negative, the two goods are complements.

**Multi-product revenue application:** if a firm sells two related goods (like a food truck selling burgers and fries), changing the price of one good affects revenue from both goods. The total change in revenue depends on both the own price elasticity of the good whose price changed and the cross price elasticity between the two goods.

Change in Revenue = [Rx times (1 + Ex) + Ry times (Ey)] times percent change in Px

Where Rx and Ry are the current revenues from each good, Ex is the own price elasticity of the good whose price changed, and Ey is the cross price elasticity of the other good with respect to that price change.

### Income Elasticity of Demand

Measures how responsive quantity demanded is to a change in consumer income.

Formula: Income elasticity = (dQ/dM) times (M/Q)

**Interpretation:**

- Positive income elasticity: the good is a **normal good** (demand rises as income rises)
- Negative income elasticity: the good is an **inferior good** (demand falls as income rises)

### Other Elasticities

The same logic (percent change in quantity divided by percent change in some driver) can be applied to nearly any variable that affects demand:

- **Own advertising elasticity:** percent change in consumption of X divided by percent change in advertising spent on X.
- **Cross advertising elasticity:** percent change in consumption of X divided by percent change in advertising spent on a related good Y.
- Other examples mentioned in lecture: elasticity with respect to distance traveled to buy a good, or elasticity with respect to wait times.

### Elasticities from a Linear Demand Function

Given a linear demand function Qx-d = alpha-zero + alpha-x times Px + alpha-y times Py + alpha-M times M + alpha-H times H, you can compute any elasticity at a specific point by taking the relevant coefficient and multiplying by the ratio of that variable's value to Qx:

- Own price elasticity = alpha-x times (Px / Qx)
- Cross price elasticity = alpha-y times (Py / Qx)
- Income elasticity = alpha-M times (M / Qx)

**Worked example logic:** Qx-d = 100 minus 3Px + 4Py minus 0.01M + 2Ax. At Px = $25, Py = $35, M = $20,000, Ax = 50, first compute Qx = 100 minus 3(25) + 4(35) minus 0.01(20,000) + 2(50) = 65 units. Then own price elasticity = negative 3 times (25/65) = negative 1.15 (elastic). Cross price elasticity = 4 times (35/65) = 2.15 (substitutes). Income elasticity = negative 0.01 times (20,000/65) = negative 3.08 (inferior good).

### Elasticities from a Log-Linear (Nonlinear) Demand Function

If quantity demanded is not linearly related to the explanatory variables, a log-linear specification is used: ln(Qx-d) = beta-zero + beta-x times ln(Px) + beta-y times ln(Py) + beta-M times ln(M) + beta-H times ln(H)

**Key shortcut:** in this log-linear form, each coefficient IS the elasticity directly, and it is constant at every point (unlike the linear demand case above). Own price elasticity = beta-x. Cross price elasticity = beta-y. Income elasticity = beta-M.

**Connection to regression (Lecture 1 callback):** if you run an OLS regression on logged variables (ln Y = a + b times ln X) the estimated coefficient b-hat is directly the elasticity of Y with respect to X. This is why economists often log transform variables before running a regression when they want to estimate an elasticity.

**Worked example logic:** ln(Qd) = 10 minus 1.2 ln(Px) + 3 ln(R) minus 2 ln(Ay), where R is rainfall. The coefficient on rainfall is 3, so a 10 percent increase in rainfall leads to a 30 percent increase in demand (3 times 10).

## Formula Cheat Sheet

**Profit and Cost**

- Accounting profit = total revenue minus explicit costs
- Economic profit = total revenue minus explicit costs minus implicit (opportunity) costs

**Time Value of Money**

- FV = (1 + i)^n times PV
- PV = FV / (1 + i)^n
- NPV = [present value of all benefits] minus [present value of all costs]
- Perpetuity PV = CF / i
- Firm value with constant growth = pi-zero times (1 + i) / (i minus g)

**Marginal Analysis**

- Net benefit N(Q) = B(Q) minus C(Q)
- Optimal Q is where MB(Q) = MC(Q), equivalently where MNB(Q) = 0

**Demand and Supply**

- Linear demand: Qd = beta-zero + beta-x Px + beta-r Pr + beta-M M + beta-H H
- Linear supply: Qs = beta-zero + beta-x Px + beta-r Pr + beta-w W + beta-H H
- Equilibrium: set Qd = Qs, solve for P, then solve for Q
- Consumer surplus or producer surplus (triangle) = 0.5 times base times height

**Elasticity**

- Own price elasticity = (dQ/dP) times (P/Q)
- Cross price elasticity = (dQx/dPy) times (Py/Qx)
- Income elasticity = (dQ/dM) times (M/Q)
- From a linear demand function, elasticity at a point = coefficient times (variable value / Q)
- From a log-linear demand function, the coefficient IS the elasticity directly (constant everywhere)
- If |elasticity| greater than 1: elastic. If |elasticity| less than 1: inelastic. If equal to 1: unitary
- Elastic demand: price up means revenue down. Inelastic demand: price up means revenue up

**Market Failures**

- Negative externality: free market overproduces
- Positive externality: free market underproduces
- Public good (non-rival, non-excludable): free market underprovides (free rider problem)
- Common resource (rival, non-excludable): free market overuses (tragedy of the commons)
- Adverse selection: asymmetric information causes quality sellers to exit the market
