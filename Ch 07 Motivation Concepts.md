- **Contemporary motivation theories complement each other** by addressing different aspects of why and how people are motivated at work.

## Motivation Defined

- **Motivation consists of three key elements: intensity, direction, and persistence.**
- **Intensity** refers to how hard you try to achieve a goal, but high intensity alone is not enough unless it is properly focused.
- **Direction** ensures that your effort is aimed toward organizational goals, making your hard work beneficial and aligned with what is needed.
- **Persistence** measures how long you can sustain your effort, indicating your ability to keep working toward a goal until it is accomplished.
- Motivation can be both a stable personality trait and a temporary state that varies depending on the situation.

## Classic Theories of Motivation

- **Maslow’s hierarchy of needs theory** proposes that human motivation is driven by a series of hierarchical needs, starting from basic physiological needs, followed by safety, social, esteem, and culminating in self-actualization needs.
- **McClelland’s theory of needs** identifies three primary motivators for individuals: the need for achievement (nAch), the need for power (nPow), and the need for affiliation (nAff), suggesting that people are motivated by different combinations of these needs.
- Both theories are foundational in understanding motivation, but their validity is debated, and they should be applied with caution compared to more contemporary, evidence-based approaches.

## Hierarchy of Needs Theory

- **Maslow’s hierarchy of needs identifies five levels of human motivation:** physiological (basic bodily needs), safety-security (protection from harm), social-belongingness (affection and acceptance), esteem (self-respect and recognition), and self-actualization (personal growth and fulfillment).
- **A proposed sixth need, intrinsic values, exists at the highest level but lacks widespread acceptance.**
- **Needs are often depicted as a pyramid or ladder, with each need becoming dominant as the previous is satisfied, though Maslow did not originally present it this way; the theory is widely recognized for its intuitive logic but lacks strong empirical support and does not fully explain workplace motivation.**

## McClelland’s Theory of Needs

- **McClelland’s theory of needs identifies three primary motivating factors:** need for achievement (nAch), need for power (nPow), and need for affiliation (nAff).
- **Need for achievement (nAch)** is the drive to excel or meet standards; high achievers prefer tasks with a moderate probability of success (about 50%) because these provide both challenge and satisfaction.
- **High nAch individuals** tend to have more positive moods, greater task interest, and excel in high-stakes job situations.
- **Need for power (nPow)** is the desire to influence or have an impact on others, not necessarily to be dictatorial.
- **Need for affiliation (nAff)** is the desire for friendly, close interpersonal relationships; teams with high nAff members often perform best, communicate openly, and have less conflict.
- **Measuring these needs is challenging** because behaviors can satisfy multiple needs and different behaviors can fulfill the same need, making the theory difficult to apply objectively in practice.

## Contemporary Theories of Motivation: Content-Based

- **Content-based theories of motivation focus on fundamental motives and individual differences that drive motivation in all people.**
- **Self-determination theory** emphasizes the importance of autonomy, competence, and relatedness as core psychological needs that, when satisfied, enhance motivation and well-being.
- **Regulatory-focus theory** distinguishes between two motivational orientations: promotion focus (striving for growth and accomplishments) and prevention focus (striving for safety and responsibility), highlighting how individuals pursue goals differently based on their focus.
- **Job engagement theory** centers on the degree to which individuals are psychologically invested in their work, with higher engagement leading to greater motivation and performance.
- Content-based theories are distinct from context-based theories (which focus on external sources of motivation like reinforcement and social learning) and process-based theories (which examine how people choose and pursue goals, such as expectancy and goal-setting theories).

## Self-Determination Theory

- **Self-determination theory (SDT) emphasizes that both the amount and the quality of motivation affect well-being and performance.** Motivation is most effective when it is autonomous (driven by personal interest or values) rather than controlled (driven by external pressures or obligations).
- **Autonomous motivation leads to better performance and psychological well-being,** while controlled motivation results in short-term compliance but is linked to higher stress, lower engagement, and increased turnover intentions.
- **Cognitive Evaluation Theory (CET), a sub-theory of SDT, states that extrinsic rewards (like pay) can reduce intrinsic motivation.** When people are paid for tasks they previously enjoyed, their interest may decrease because the activity feels less voluntary and more obligatory.
- **Intrinsic motivation enhances the quality of work, while extrinsic incentives tend to increase the quantity of work.** However, direct performance-based incentives can weaken intrinsic motivation.
- **SDT identifies three basic psychological needs that drive motivation: autonomy, competence, and relatedness.** Autonomy is the need to feel in control of one’s actions, competence is the need to feel effective and skilled, and relatedness is the need to feel connected to others.
- **Of the three needs, autonomy is most important for positive attitudes and feelings, while competence is most predictive of performance.** Relatedness is crucial for sustaining motivation, especially in remote or hybrid work settings.
- **Remote work can increase autonomy and competence but may reduce relatedness, leading to feelings of isolation and decreased intrinsic motivation.** Satisfying all three needs is essential for sustainable motivation.
- **Organizations should design jobs that support autonomy, competence, and relatedness,** offering recognition, opportunities for growth, and fostering meaningful interpersonal connections without forcing interactions.
- **Recognition programs, like the STAR Award, help create a culture of support and acknowledgment,** which can enhance motivation and organizational commitment.

## Myth or Science?

- **Purposeful work is often used as a motivational strategy**, with organizations emphasizing the broader impact of employees’ roles to inspire engagement and productivity.
- **Finding work meaningful leads to higher productivity, job satisfaction, and retention**, but overemphasizing purpose can backfire if daily tasks feel disconnected from the promised greater mission.
- **Routine and mundane tasks are common in most jobs**, and even in high-stakes environments, such as UN peacekeeping, employees report frequent boredom.
- **Superordinate framing is an effective intervention**, where employees connect routine tasks to larger organizational goals, helping them find meaning in everyday work.
- **Employees who accept boredom and adjust their expectations** by focusing on immediate, achievable goals are better able to stay motivated and see their contributions as part of a larger purpose.
- **Failure to cope with boredom or routine tasks increases disengagement and turnover**, while acceptance and reframing can enhance motivation and job satisfaction.

## Regulatory Focus Theory

- **Regulatory focus theory identifies two main motivational orientations:** promotion focus (striving for advancement and accomplishment) and prevention focus (striving to fulfill duties and avoid negative outcomes).
- **Individuals can shift between promotion and prevention focus** depending on the situation, rather than being permanently fixed in one category.
- **Promotion focus leads to behaviors aimed at achieving gains**, such as engaging in activities that move you closer to your goals (e.g., reading class materials to prepare for an exam).
- **Prevention focus leads to behaviors aimed at avoiding losses**, such as refraining from distractions that could hinder goal achievement (e.g., avoiding video games while studying).
- **The effectiveness of each focus depends on the desired outcome:** promotion focus is linked to higher task performance, citizenship behavior, and innovation, while prevention focus is linked to better safety performance.
- **Optimal goal pursuit often involves both promotion and prevention strategies**, tailored to the demands of the environment and specific goals.
- **Managers play a key role in shaping regulatory focus within organizations**, as their approach can influence and spread to employees, affecting overall motivation and behavior.

## Job Engagement Theory

- **Job engagement** refers to how much you invest your physical, cognitive, and emotional energy into your work tasks, leading to higher effectiveness and personal fulfillment.
- Job engagement is distinct from employee engagement: job engagement focuses on energy and involvement during work tasks, while employee engagement is a general positive attitude toward the job overall.
- High job engagement is characterized by vigor, dedication, and absorption, where you may lose track of time and become fully immersed in your work.
- Organizations benefit from job engagement, as it predicts higher task performance and citizenship behavior, while disengagement leads to significant productivity losses globally.
- Recent trends show a decline in engagement, with employees feeling less connected to their employers and organizational missions.
- Key predictors of job engagement include proactive personality, conscientiousness, extraversion, and especially the tendency to experience positive moods and emotions (positive affect).

## Contemporary Theories of Motivation: Context-Based

- **Reinforcement theory and social learning theory focus on how context and environment influence motivation**, rather than just internal needs or individual differences.
- **Reinforcement theory** explains motivation as a result of external rewards and punishments, suggesting that behavior is shaped by its consequences.
- **Social learning theory** highlights the importance of observing others and modeling their behaviors, showing that people can be motivated by watching and imitating those around them.
- These context-based theories differ from content-based theories, which emphasize universal psychological needs and individual differences, by stressing the role of external factors in shaping motivation.

## Reinforcement Theory

- **Reinforcement theory states that behavior is shaped by its consequences, focusing on external factors (the environment) rather than internal cognitive processes.**
- **A reinforcer is any consequence that, when it immediately follows a behavior, increases the likelihood that the behavior will be repeated.** Positive reinforcement (like compliments) encourages repetition of desired behaviors, while negative reinforcement or punishment discourages undesired behaviors.
- **Operant conditioning, a concept from B. F. Skinner, explains that people learn to behave in certain ways to obtain rewards or avoid punishments, associating specific stimuli with responses regardless of conscious awareness.**
- **Skinner’s radical behaviorism rejects internal states (thoughts, feelings) as causes of behavior, emphasizing that behavior follows stimuli in an automatic way.**
- **Rewards are most effective when they immediately follow the desired behavior, and behaviors not rewarded or punished are less likely to be repeated.**
- **Reinforcement theory is often used in organizations through reward and discipline policies, but it contrasts with other motivation theories that consider internal psychological factors.**
- **Unlike reinforcement theory, cognitive evaluation theory suggests that rewards can sometimes decrease intrinsic motivation, showing that reinforcement is not always straightforward or universally effective.**
- **Relying solely on rewards and punishments for motivation is generally not desirable, as it ignores the complex psychological factors that also influence behavior.**

## Social Learning Theory

- **Social learning theory states that you learn through both observation and direct experience.**
- **Behavior is influenced not just by consequences, but by how you perceive and interpret those consequences.**
- **You learn by observing others, remembering their actions, and then reproducing those behaviors yourself.**
- **Behaviors are more likely to be repeated if they are reinforced, such as receiving positive feedback from a role model like a coach or supervisor.**
- **Supervisors serve as important models in organizations, and employees often imitate their behaviors—including pragmatic, autonomous, or even unethical actions—if they observe supervisors acting that way.**

## An Ethical Choice

- **Employee monitoring is widespread**, with about 80% of companies using technologies to track activities such as keystrokes, mouse movements, and Internet use, aiming to ensure productivity.
- **Invasive monitoring can negatively impact employees**, leading to disengagement and distrust toward supervisors, which can reduce motivation and harm workplace relationships.
- **Some companies are shifting to more interactive monitoring**, using technology to facilitate regular check-ins, feedback, and goal clarification through virtual meetings and chat channels, which can foster engagement.
- **Ethical use of monitoring technology requires transparency**: employees should be informed about monitoring, understand its purpose, and know what behaviors are considered inappropriate.
- **Clear communication and meaningful feedback** are essential; distinguishing between legitimate breaks and misuse of time helps employees perceive monitoring as fair and increases acceptance.
- **Performance reviews and active engagement** are more effective for motivation and productivity than passive or micromanaging surveillance.

## Contemporary Theories of Motivation: Process-Based

- **Expectancy theory** explains motivation as a process where you decide how much effort to put in based on your expectations that your effort will lead to good performance, and that performance will lead to desired outcomes.
- **Goal-setting theory** emphasizes that you are more motivated when you set specific, challenging goals and receive feedback on your progress toward those goals.
- **Self-efficacy theory** focuses on your belief in your own ability to succeed at a task, which directly influences your motivation, effort, and persistence.

## Expectancy Theory

- **Expectancy theory explains motivation as a function of three key relationships:** expectancy (effort leads to performance), instrumentality (performance leads to rewards), and valence (rewards satisfy personal goals).
- **Expectancy** is your belief that putting in effort will result in successful performance; if you think your effort won’t make a difference, motivation drops.
- **Instrumentality** is your belief that good performance will actually lead to desired rewards; if you doubt that high performance will be recognized or rewarded, you’re less likely to be motivated.
- **Valence** is how much you value the rewards offered; if the reward doesn’t matter to you, even if you believe you can achieve it, your motivation will be low.
- The theory helps explain why some employees do only the minimum required—if any of the three links (expectancy, instrumentality, valence) are weak, motivation suffers.
- The example of a parking spot as a reward illustrates how individual differences in valence (how much the reward is valued) and perceptions of fairness or achievability (expectancy and instrumentality) affect motivation.

## Goal-Setting Theory

- **Goal-setting theory** posits that setting goals significantly enhances motivation and performance by directing attention, mobilizing effort, encouraging persistence, and facilitating strategy development.
- **Goal origins** (self-set vs. supervisor-set) impact psychological outcomes: self-set goals can boost enthusiasm, while supervisor-set goals may increase anxiety and reduce organizational citizenship behaviors.
- **Specific and difficult goals** lead to higher performance than vague or easy goals because they clarify expectations and require greater effort; for example, “apply to 10–15 jobs per week” is more effective than “start applying to jobs.”
- **Goal framing** (maintenance vs. attainment, minimal vs. maximal standards) influences satisfaction and accomplishment, with maximal standards often yielding greater satisfaction.
- **Potential downsides** of challenging goals include cognitive overload, burnout, and ethical lapses, especially when multiple demanding goals are present or rewards are tied to performance.
- **Key factors influencing goal-setting effectiveness** include:
    - **Goal commitment**: Success depends on believing in and wanting to achieve the goal, which is strengthened by participation, perceived attainability, and personal investment.
    - **Task characteristics**: Goal setting works best for simple, independent tasks; for complex or interdependent tasks, group-centered or cooperative goals are more effective.
    - **Feedback**: Regular, especially self-generated, feedback improves performance by highlighting progress and guiding behavior.
    - **Goal orientation**: Mastery orientation (focus on learning) leads to better outcomes than performance-avoid (fear of failure) or performance-prove (desire to demonstrate ability) orientations.
    - **Goal conflict**: Competing goals increase stress and can harm performance; managing conflict involves recognizing the conflict, focusing on actionable steps, and fostering collaboration.
- **Management by Objectives (MBO)** is a systematic approach to goal setting involving specific, measurable goals, participation, explicit time frames, and feedback, with objectives cascading through organizational levels.
- **MBO failures** often result from unrealistic expectations, lack of top management commitment, or failure to link rewards to goal achievement.
- **Goal setting and ethics**: Emphasizing goal attainment, especially with monetary rewards or under time pressure, can increase unethical behavior, particularly when individuals are depleted or focused on proving themselves rather than mastering tasks.

## Self-Efficacy Theory

- **Self-efficacy is your belief in your ability to perform a task**, and higher self-efficacy leads to greater confidence and motivation to succeed, especially in challenging situations.
- People with high self-efficacy are more likely to persist and try harder when facing difficulties, while those with low self-efficacy may give up more easily.
- **Intelligence, conscientiousness, and emotional stability are linked to higher self-efficacy**, suggesting that self-efficacy may be more important for motivation and performance than previously thought.
- Self-efficacy is not just about initial motivation; it also helps you maintain motivation over time, as your perceived progress can further influence your self-efficacy and effort.
- **A positive spiral can occur**: high self-efficacy leads to greater engagement and performance, which in turn boosts self-efficacy even more.
- **Combining goal-setting with self-efficacy increases performance**: Supportive managers who set challenging goals can help employees develop higher self-efficacy and set higher goals for themselves.
- **Four main ways to increase self-efficacy**:
    1. Enactive mastery: Gaining experience through practice.
    2. Vicarious modeling: Observing others successfully perform the task.
    3. Verbal persuasion: Receiving encouragement and reassurance.
    4. Arousal: Getting energized or “psyched up” to approach tasks positively.
- **The Pygmalion effect is a form of self-fulfilling prophecy** where higher expectations from others can lead to improved performance, as people tend to live up to the expectations set for them.
- Strong leader–subordinate relationships can enhance the Pygmalion effect, and leadership potential can often be found in unexpected individuals, not just those who initially appear to be leaders.

## Organizational Justice

- **Organizational justice** is about your perception of fairness in the workplace, which strongly influences your trust, commitment, and motivation.
- There are four main types of organizational justice: **distributive, procedural, informational, and interpersonal** justice.
- **Distributive justice** focuses on the perceived fairness of outcomes or resource distribution (such as pay, promotions, or workload).
- **Procedural justice** concerns the fairness of the processes and methods used to determine outcomes.
- **Interactional justice** (which includes informational and interpersonal justice) relates to the fairness of how people are treated and the quality of information provided during decision-making processes.
- Each type of justice addresses a unique aspect of fairness, shaping how you and others respond to workplace decisions and leadership.

## Equity Theory

- **Equity theory explains employee satisfaction by comparing the ratio of outcomes (like pay, promotions, recognition) to inputs (such as effort, experience, education) with those of others in similar roles.** If the ratios are perceived as equal, employees feel fairly treated; if not, they perceive inequity and unfairness.
- **Perceived inequity can lead employees to take actions such as changing their effort, altering outcomes, distorting perceptions of themselves or others, choosing a different comparison person, or leaving the job.** These are the six typical responses to perceived inequity.
- **Employees differ in their sensitivity to equity, with some feeling more entitled and others more benevolent, which affects how they respond to perceived fairness or unfairness.**
- **Organizational justice extends beyond equity theory, focusing on how employees perceive fairness in treatment by authorities and adherence to workplace rules and principles.** This includes both passive and active forms of perceived injustice, such as executives taking more than their fair share.
- **Research consistently shows that perceptions of justice are crucial for strong employee–organization relationships, especially during periods of crisis or uncertainty.** Employees judge fairness by comparing their treatment to their own standards and expectations.

## Distributive Justice

- **Distributive justice focuses on the perceived fairness of outcomes** such as pay and recognition that employees receive in the workplace.
- **Outcomes can be allocated in various ways**, including equal distribution, need-based distribution, or based on individual contributions, but employees generally view outcomes as most fair when distributed equitably.
- **Equity theory suggests employees compare their outcome–input ratios to others** to assess fairness, but these judgments are often influenced by emotional reactions rather than purely rational calculations.
- **Emotional responses to perceived injustice can be strong**, as illustrated by contract workers at tech companies like Google, who feel undervalued despite their critical contributions.
- **The type of reward matters**, with most people placing greater importance on pay differences over other forms of recognition or challenging assignments.

## Procedural Justice

- **Procedural justice focuses on the fairness of the processes used to distribute outcomes, not just the outcomes themselves.**
- Employees perceive procedures as fairer when they have a voice in the decision-making process and when decisions are made consistently, without bias, using accurate information, considering those affected, and allowing for appeals.
- When employees understand and trust the fairness of the process, they are more likely to accept unfavorable outcomes, especially if they had input or the process was transparent.
- The perceived fairness of procedures also depends on practical aspects, such as the time required for the process; both lengthy and quick processes can be seen as fair depending on context.
- The use of AI in decision-making aims to reduce bias and increase fairness, but employees may view AI tools as impersonal or unable to consider individual circumstances, sometimes perceiving increased bias.
- Real-world examples, such as the Dutch government’s use of biased algorithms for fraud detection, show that AI can unintentionally increase discrimination, especially when not properly monitored.
- Involving affected parties in the development and evaluation of AI-assisted procedures can help reduce bias and build trust in the fairness of these systems.

## Interactional Justice

- **Interactional justice** includes two key types: informational justice and interpersonal justice, both focusing on fairness in how employees are treated during interactions.
- **Informational justice** is about how thoroughly and honestly managers explain key decisions and keep employees informed about important matters. Employees feel more fairly treated when managers are transparent and candid, especially during difficult situations like layoffs.
- Mishandling communication, particularly when delivering bad news, can lead to negative consequences such as employee dissatisfaction and reputational harm through social media or review platforms.
- **Interpersonal justice** refers to the degree to which employees are treated with dignity and respect by their supervisors. This is demonstrated through attentiveness, politeness, and sincerity in direct interactions.
- When managers treat some employees fairly and others unfairly, perceptions of interpersonal justice decrease, especially among employees who are highly committed and deeply involved in the organization.

## Justice Outcomes

- **Perceptions of justice in the workplace significantly impact employee attitudes and behaviors**, with fair treatment linked to higher performance (up to 26% improvement) and retention (up to 27% improvement).
- **All four types of justice—distributive, procedural, informational, and interpersonal—are associated with positive outcomes**: distributive and procedural justice are more closely tied to task performance, while informational and interpersonal justice are more related to citizenship behaviors like helping coworkers.
- **Fair treatment reduces counterproductive behaviors** such as shirking duties, and is also linked to better physiological outcomes, including improved sleep and health.
- **Justice enhances organizational commitment and trust in supervisors**, reducing uncertainty and fear of exploitation, and fostering positive emotions that encourage citizenship behaviors.
- **Perceived unfairness can lead to work withdrawal**, where employees reduce their emotional, cognitive, or behavioral involvement in their jobs, often subtly through lower effort or avoidance of extra responsibilities.
- **Quiet quitting is a form of work withdrawal**, where employees meet only the basic job requirements and refrain from extra-role activities, often as a response to feeling overextended, underappreciated, or unfairly treated.
- **Distributive injustice, especially regarding pay, is a common driver of withdrawal behaviors**; when employees perceive inequity in rewards compared to their contributions or peers, their motivation and engagement decline.
- **Even small discrepancies in compensation can predict reduced motivation and increased withdrawal**, as employees psychologically detach when they feel the link between effort and outcome is weakened.

## Integrating Contemporary Theories of Motivation

- **Contemporary motivation theories are complementary and interconnected**, with each theory addressing different aspects of motivation and influencing one another within an integrated framework.
- **Expectancy theory serves as a central model**, highlighting that your effort is influenced by both personal goals (goal-setting theory) and available opportunities. You are more likely to exert high effort if you believe your effort will lead to good performance, performance will lead to rewards, and rewards will satisfy your personal goals.
- **Ability and fairness are crucial for motivation**: For your effort to translate into good performance, you must have the necessary ability and perceive the performance appraisal system as fair and objective.
- **Performance–reward relationship depends on perceived fairness**: Motivation is higher when you believe rewards are based on actual performance rather than unrelated factors like seniority or favoritism.
- **Intrinsic motivation and rewards**: According to cognitive evaluation theory, tying rewards directly to performance can sometimes reduce intrinsic motivation, but this effect may be weaker when incentives are clearly linked to performance.
- **Rewards must align with personal goals and needs**: Motivation is strongest when rewards for high performance satisfy your dominant needs and are consistent with your individual goals.
- **Achievement motivation, job design, and reinforcement theory add depth**: High achievers (those with a high need for achievement) are motivated more by personal responsibility, feedback, and moderate risk than by organizational rewards or performance assessments.
- **Reinforcement theory emphasizes the role of rewards**: When you see rewards as a direct result of good performance, they reinforce and encourage continued high performance.
- **Equity and organizational justice theories highlight fairness**: You judge the fairness of your outcomes (like pay) not only by comparing them to others but also by evaluating the fairness of procedures and the treatment you receive from supervisors.
- **Practical applications, such as competitions to increase engagement and achieve organizational goals, illustrate how these theories can be used together to motivate employees effectively.**

## Toward a Better World

- **Land O’Lakes set ambitious sustainability goals**, including reducing food waste by 30%, cutting landfill waste from operations by 50%, and lowering emissions by 25% by 2030.
- **Employee engagement was central to achieving these goals**, with the company involving employees at all levels, especially those in production who have unique insights into waste issues.
- **Educational initiatives were implemented** to inform employees about the impacts of food loss and waste, such as climate change, greenhouse gas emissions, global hunger, and resource wastage.
- **Leadership conducted plant walkthroughs** to directly engage with employees and identify specific points where food waste occurred.
- **A four-week challenge, “Solve the Mystery of the Lost Food,” encouraged employees to submit waste reduction ideas**, resulting in nearly 300 suggestions and incorporating gamification elements like weekly prizes and recognition.
- **Employee-generated solutions led to significant results**, such as using spatulas in flan production, which reduced waste by 74% and saved $3,500 annually.
- **The initiative fostered a culture of sustainability and continuous improvement**, with employees actively participating and seeing the impact of their contributions.
- **Land O’Lakes demonstrated effective corporate social responsibility practices** by increasing self-determination, providing rewards and recognition, and setting meaningful goals, leading to both environmental and financial benefits.
