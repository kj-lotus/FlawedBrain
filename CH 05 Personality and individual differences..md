## The Politicization of the Workplace

- **Political polarization is increasingly affecting workplaces**, influencing hiring decisions and workplace interactions, with evidence showing that political bias can impact both hiring and promotion, sometimes more than race or gender.
- **Employers often use social media (cybervetting) to assess candidates’ political affiliations**, despite the absence of federal laws protecting political values as a class, leading to potential bias in employment practices.
- **Organizations are implementing policies to regulate political expression at work**, such as Meta’s Respectful Communication Policy, but these can lead to employee dissatisfaction or turnover if perceived as suppressing beliefs, as seen when Basecamp lost a third of its staff after restricting political talk.
- **Workplaces with greater political diversity tend to perform better**, with studies showing that politically homogeneous teams experience slower growth, while inclusive teams with diverse values achieve better outcomes.
- **Both employers and employees are increasingly seeking values alignment**, but there is a risk of crossing into political bias; companies like Patagonia hire for environmental values, but must be cautious not to discriminate based on political beliefs.
- **Banning political discussions is less effective than fostering a respectful culture**, as it is difficult to define what counts as “political,” and encouraging respect for differing values leads to healthier workplace dynamics.
- **Individual differences such as values and personality traits significantly influence workplace behavior and success**, with the Big Five personality traits (extraversion, conscientiousness, openness, agreeableness, emotional stability) providing a key framework for understanding these differences, alongside other traits like core self-evaluations and proactive personality.
- **The concept of “fit” is central**, referring to how well a person’s values, traits, and interests align with the roles, relationships, and culture of a workplace, impacting both individual and organizational outcomes.

## Linking Individuals to the Workplace

- **Person–job fit** refers to how well your personality, skills, and abilities match the requirements of a specific job. Managers traditionally focused on this to ensure you could perform the tasks needed for a particular role.
- **Person–organization fit** is about how well your values and personality align with the overall culture and values of the organization. This fit is increasingly important because it affects your engagement, likelihood to stay with the company, and ability to contribute to the organization’s broader mission.
- Hiring for person–organization fit can increase employee engagement and reduce turnover, as employees whose values align with the organization are more likely to stay and be productive.
- Companies like Rising Tide Car Wash demonstrate the benefits of considering both person–job and person–organization fit, especially by valuing diversity and recognizing that the qualities that make someone successful can differ greatly between organizations.

## Person–Job Fit

- **Holland’s personality–job fit theory** asserts that job satisfaction and the likelihood of leaving a position depend on how well your personality matches your job.
- The theory identifies **six personality types** and links them to specific occupational profiles, using tools like the Vocational Preference Inventory to assess interests.
- **Person–job fit** is a strong predictor of job satisfaction, organizational commitment, and intentions to quit, with research supporting its effectiveness.
- **Cultural differences** impact the importance of person–job fit: it is more significant in individualistic cultures, where job tailoring increases satisfaction, but less so in collectivistic cultures, where fitting into existing roles is valued.
- **Age influences job fit strategies**: older employees benefit more from autonomy and opportunities to align their jobs with their strengths and interests, as they have greater self-awareness compared to younger workers.

## Person–Organization Fit

- **Person–organization fit** is the compatibility between you and an organization, based on shared values or the fulfillment of mutual needs, rather than just matching your skills to a specific job.
- Organizations with dynamic, changing environments prioritize hiring individuals whose personalities and values align with the overall organizational culture, enabling flexibility and teamwork.
- Extraverts are likely to thrive in organizations that emphasize relationships and social interaction, illustrating how personality traits can align with organizational values.
- Companies like JP Morgan Chase are adopting holistic hiring practices, focusing on whether candidates fit into the team and represent organizational values, rather than strictly on academic background or specific job skills.
- A strong person–organization fit leads to higher job satisfaction, greater commitment to the organization, and lower employee turnover.

## Toward a Better World

- **Uber’s early leadership under Travis Kalanick fostered a toxic, win-at-all-costs culture**, which contributed to numerous scandals and negative public perception, including lawsuits and allegations of unethical behavior.
- **Dara Khosrowshahi became CEO in 2017 with a mandate to reform Uber’s culture and image**, distancing the company from Kalanick’s influence and focusing on responsible, sustainable growth rather than unchecked expansion.
- **Khosrowshahi replaced Uber’s original, vague corporate values with clear, actionable ones**, involving over 1,000 employees in the process and emphasizing principles like “We do the right thing. Period.” and “We celebrate differences,” aiming to shift organizational behavior.
- **Changing values on paper is not sufficient; real change requires living those values**, as organizational behavior is shaped by the values practiced, not just stated.
- **Uber continues to face challenges with its reputation**, particularly regarding driver compensation and benefits, but has made progress with CSR and sustainability initiatives, such as commitments to eliminate emissions on Uber Eats deliveries by 2040 and reduce plastic waste by 2030.
- **Leadership and employee engagement are crucial for successful CSR**, as core values must be integrated into the company’s operations and culture to achieve meaningful and lasting change.

## Other Dimensions of Fit

- **Person–group fit** refers to how well you align with your team, which is crucial in team-based environments because team dynamics strongly influence work outcomes.
- **Person–supervisor fit** focuses on the compatibility between you and your supervisor; poor fit here can decrease job satisfaction and performance, while congruence (such as shared attitudes like cynicism) can predict job performance.
- All types of fit—including person–job, person–organization, person–group, and person–supervisor—are collectively known as **person–environment fit** and can predict your work attitudes.
- The importance of different fit dimensions varies by culture: person–organization and person–job fit are most predictive of positive work attitudes and performance in North America, less so in Europe, and least in East Asia.
- Leaders can enhance your perception of fit by matching your individual differences to the job, offering realistic job previews, providing training and socialization, and giving ongoing support.

## Personality

- **Personality refers to the unique set of characteristics, traits, and behaviors that define how individuals think, feel, and act.**
- **Personality is measured using various tools and assessments, such as questionnaires and psychological tests, which help identify individual differences.**
- **Factors that shape personality include biological influences, environmental factors, and life experiences, all of which contribute to the diversity of personalities found in the workplace.**

## What Is Personality?

- **Personality refers to the sum of ways in which you react to and interact with the world**, typically described through measurable traits.
- **Personality traits are enduring characteristics** that you consistently exhibit across different situations and over time, such as being outgoing, hardworking, or aloof.
- **The consistency and frequency of a trait across situations make it more significant** in defining your personality.
- **Culture shapes how personality traits are described and expressed**, with some traits being universal and others unique or absent in certain cultures (e.g., interpersonal relatedness in Chinese contexts, less emphasis on openness).
- **Awareness of cultural differences is important** when interpreting personality traits in people from different backgrounds.

## Measuring Personality

- **Personality assessments are increasingly used in organizational settings for hiring decisions**, not just for managers and executives but also for full-time and hourly employees.
- **Self-report surveys are the most common method for measuring personality**, but applicants often rate themselves higher on desirable traits when they know scores will be used for hiring, leading to potential faking.
- **Test design strategies to reduce faking include forced-choice formats and faking warnings**, which inform applicants about the risks and consequences of dishonest answers, such as detection, disqualification, or poor job fit.
- **Observer-ratings surveys provide independent assessments of personality**, and while self-reports and observer-ratings are strongly correlated, observer-ratings often predict job success better, especially when the observer knows the individual well.
- **Combining self-reports and observer-ratings yields better predictions of job performance** than relying on a single type of evaluation.
- **Advancements in technology and AI, including machine learning, have improved personality assessment** by selecting informative questions, detecting faking, analyzing language use on social media, and scoring job interviews.

## Personality Frameworks

- **The Myers–Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) and the Big Five Model are the most widely used personality frameworks**, both aiming to describe a person's overall personality by examining different facets.
- **The MBTI lacks strong empirical support**, meaning research does not consistently validate its effectiveness or accuracy in predicting behavior, making it less reliable for evidence-based practice.
- **The Big Five Model is supported by decades of research**, providing a solid, evidence-based foundation for understanding and predicting personality and behavior.
- **The Dark Triad framework focuses on specific negative personality traits** (notably, narcissism, Machiavellianism, and psychopathy) but does not offer a comprehensive view of overall personality.
- **Personality frameworks help predict behavior by categorizing traits**, such as low agreeableness leading to rudeness or directness, which can affect workplace interactions.

## The Myers–Briggs Type Indicator

- **The Myers–Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) is a widely used personality assessment tool** that classifies individuals based on four dichotomies: Extraverted (E) vs. Intraverted (I), Sensing (S) vs. Intuitive (N), Thinking (T) vs. Feeling (F), and Judging (J) vs. Perceiving (P).
- **Each dichotomy describes contrasting personality traits:** Extraverts are sociable and assertive, while intraverts are quiet and shy; sensing types focus on details and routine, while intuitives look at the big picture; thinking types use logic, while feeling types rely on values and emotions; judging types prefer structure, while perceiving types are flexible.
- **Personality types are formed by combining one trait from each pair** (e.g., INTJ: Intraverted, Intuitive, Thinking, Judging), with each combination associated with specific characteristics (e.g., INTJs are visionary, independent, and determined).
- **The MBTI faces significant criticisms:** it was developed subjectively without strong scientific backing, lacks empirical support for its validity, forces individuals into binary categories, produces inconsistent results upon retesting, and requires complex interpretation that can lead to errors.
- **Despite its popularity, the MBTI does not predict job performance or important organizational outcomes,** making it unsuitable for personnel selection; the Big Five Model is recommended as a better alternative for assessing personality in organizational contexts.
- **The MBTI remains popular because its results are nonjudgmental and appealing,** offering individuals a simple and positive narrative about their personality, even though these results may not be scientifically reliable.

## The Big Five Model

- **The Big Five Model identifies five core personality dimensions:** conscientiousness, emotional stability, extraversion, openness to experience, and agreeableness, which together explain most differences in human personality and reliably predict behavior across various situations.
- **Conscientiousness is the strongest predictor of job performance:** Highly conscientious individuals are responsible, organized, and persistent, leading to higher job knowledge, performance, and organizational citizenship behaviors (OCBs), and lower counterproductive work behaviors (CWBs). However, excessive conscientiousness can result in perfectionism, reduced creativity, and diminished happiness.
- **Emotional stability (opposite of neuroticism) is linked to life and job satisfaction:** High emotional stability helps individuals handle stress, adapt to change, and avoid burnout, while low emotional stability increases the risk of CWBs, work–family conflict, and intentions to quit.
- **Extraversion provides a career advantage:** Extraverts are sociable, assertive, and experience more positive emotions, which supports leadership emergence, job satisfaction, and adaptability to career changes. However, extraverts may dominate group settings and take unnecessary risks in roles not requiring social interaction.
- **Openness to experience fosters creativity and adaptability:** Open individuals are innovative, comfortable with ambiguity, and adapt well to organizational change. While openness does not predict initial job performance, it helps maintain performance over time and reduces work–family conflict.
- **Agreeableness supports interpersonal success:** Agreeable people are cooperative and trusting, excel in customer service and team roles, and engage in more OCBs and fewer CWBs. However, high agreeableness may limit career advancement and earnings due to less assertiveness.
- **Personality traits influence occupational fit:** Openness varies most across jobs (highest in artists and professors, lowest in truck drivers), conscientiousness is highest in managers and chefs, extraversion peaks in actors and event planners, agreeableness is highest in caregivers, and neuroticism is most common in creative fields.
- **High-performance occupations tend to be personality-homogeneous:** Jobs like pilots and managers attract and reinforce traits such as emotional stability and conscientiousness, shaping both who enters and succeeds in these roles.
- **The Big Five traits are strongly linked to organizational outcomes and career paths,** but do not capture all personality dimensions relevant to organizational behavior.

## The Dark Triad

- **The Dark Triad consists of Machiavellianism, narcissism, and psychopathy, which are socially undesirable personality traits present to varying degrees in everyone.** These traits are not clinical disorders but can become more pronounced under stress and may negatively impact careers and relationships if sustained at high levels.
    
- **Machiavellianism is characterized by pragmatism, emotional detachment, and the belief that the ends justify the means.** Individuals high in Machiavellianism manipulate others, are persuasive, and may use both counterproductive (CWB) and organizational citizenship behaviors (OCB) if it benefits them. While they may achieve short-term success, they are generally not well liked and do not perform better overall in the long term.
    
- **Narcissism involves a grandiose sense of self-importance, a need for excessive admiration, arrogance, and a lack of empathy.** Narcissists are sensitive to criticism, often believe they are overqualified, and may ignore negative feedback unless rewards are involved. Narcissism is a strong predictor of increased CWB in individualistic cultures but not in collectivist cultures. Narcissists can be charismatic and are often chosen for leadership roles, with moderate levels of narcissism linked to leadership effectiveness and adaptability.
    
- **Psychopathy in organizational behavior refers to a lack of concern for others, absence of guilt or remorse, impulsivity, and willingness to use deceit.** Research on psychopathy’s impact at work is mixed: some studies find little effect on performance, while others link it to aggressive behaviors like bullying and manipulation. Primary psychopathy is marked by emotional detachment and superficial charm, while secondary psychopathy involves impulsivity and aggression, leading to poor performance and high CWB. The charm of primary psychopaths may help them advance, but ultimately undermines their effectiveness as leaders.
    
- **The effects of these traits vary by context and culture, and while they may offer short-term advantages, they often result in long-term negative consequences for individuals and organizations.**
    

## An Ethical Choice

- **Certain personality traits are linked to a higher likelihood of unethical behavior.** People low in emotional stability, agreeableness, and conscientiousness are more prone to unethical actions, whether toward individuals or organizations.
- **Traits from frameworks like the Dark Triad (dishonesty, Machiavellianism, narcissism, psychoticism) are associated with increased unethical behavior.** Individuals exhibiting these traits are more likely to act unethically.
- **The concept of the "moral self" refers to how central morality and ethicality are to your identity and motivation.** If morality is important to you, you are more likely to consider ethical implications and act ethically.
- **Moral emotions (such as anger, contempt, shame, and guilt) influence ethical behavior.** People more prone to these emotions may be driven to act ethically or unethically, while those with less emotional response may ignore ethical considerations.
- **Personality traits do not determine behavior in isolation; context and situational strength also play a significant role.** Your environment and circumstances can influence whether you act ethically, regardless of your traits.
- **Self-awareness and self-monitoring are crucial for managing ethical behavior.** If you have clear moral standards and monitor your actions, you are more likely to counteract unethical impulses, even if your personality makes you more susceptible to them.

## Other Frameworks

- The DiSC framework is a circumplex model that organizes personality traits in a circular structure, grouping similar traits closer together and dissimilar traits further apart. It identifies four primary personality types: Dominating (D), Influencing (i), Steadiness (S), and Conscientiousness (C), but emphasizes that individuals are not strictly defined by these types.
- Despite its popularity and claims of validity, the DiSC framework lacks substantial scientific research supporting its validity and reliability, indicating a need for more academic evidence.
- The HEXACO model expands the Big Five personality traits by adding a sixth dimension: Honesty-Humility (H), which captures traits like sincerity, fairness, modesty, and humility. This dimension emerged from cross-cultural studies and is associated with ethical behaviors, such as resisting cheating even when tempted.
- The addition of Honesty-Humility to personality models is significant for organizations, as it helps predict important outcomes related to ethical conduct, such as reducing dishonesty and theft in the workplace.
- The impact of personality traits on organizational outcomes depends on both the degree and combination of traits present in an individual, and it is important to consider multiple frameworks and theories rather than relying solely on one model for management decisions.

## Other Personality Attributes Relevant to OB

- **Core self-evaluation (CSE)** refers to your fundamental assessments about your own worth and capabilities, influencing how you perceive yourself and your functioning in the workplace.
- **Self-monitoring** describes your ability to adjust your behavior in response to social cues and situational demands, affecting how you interact with others and adapt to organizational environments.
- **Proactive personality** captures your tendency to take initiative, effect change, and influence your surroundings, which can lead to greater impact and success within organizations.
- These attributes are important because they help predict workplace behavior beyond traditional personality traits, offering deeper insight into how you might perform, adapt, and lead in organizational settings.

## Core Self-Evaluations (CSEs)

- **Core self-evaluations (CSEs) are fundamental beliefs about your abilities, competence, and self-worth.** Positive CSEs lead you to feel effective and in control, while negative CSEs result in self-doubt and feelings of powerlessness.
- **Positive CSEs are linked to higher job satisfaction and better job performance.** You are more likely to set ambitious goals, stay committed, persist in challenges, provide better customer service, and generate creative solutions.
- **People with high CSEs often have more successful and rapidly advancing careers.** They are also more popular among coworkers and are seen as better leaders due to their confidence and persistence.
- **Excessively high CSEs can be viewed negatively by others, especially if you appear unhelpful or self-centered in the workplace.**
- **CSEs enhance leadership effectiveness.** Leaders with high CSEs invest more in high-performing team members, build stronger relationships, and persist with strategies that improve group performance.
- **CSEs influence adaptability in diverse and international work environments.** Positive CSEs help you adjust to career changes and new cultures, compensating for differences such as language proficiency and nationality.
- **The impact of CSEs on job performance can depend on cultural values.** For example, in environments that value individualism, positive CSEs are more strongly related to job performance than in collectivist settings.

## Self-Monitoring

- **Self-monitoring is the ability to adjust your behavior to fit external, situational factors.** High self-monitors adapt their actions based on cues from their environment, while low self-monitors consistently display their true attitudes and dispositions regardless of the situation.
- **High self-monitors are more likely to receive better performance ratings, emerge as leaders, and advance in their careers.** They tend to be more mobile, get more promotions, and occupy central positions within organizations, but often show less commitment to their organizations.
- **Low self-monitors, like Riley, are seen as authentic but may struggle with workplace politics and adaptability.** High self-monitors, despite their advantages, can be perceived as inauthentic or self-serving, making self-monitoring a mixed blessing.

## Proactive Personality

- **A proactive personality involves taking initiative, identifying opportunities, and persisting until meaningful change occurs, rather than simply reacting to situations.**
- Proactive individuals often demonstrate higher job performance, creativity, autonomy, job satisfaction, organizational commitment, and engagement, which contribute to long-term career success.
- In teams, high levels of proactive personality lead to greater innovation, smoother leadership transitions, stronger team commitment, and increased information sharing and trust.
- **Drawbacks of proactivity include potential envy from peers, social undermining, and the withholding of help.**
- Proactive employees handle challenge stressors (like tight deadlines or complex projects) well and are less likely to burn out, but they are more vulnerable to hindrance stressors (such as office politics or unclear roles), which can lead to frustration, emotional exhaustion, and higher turnover intentions.
- The impact of proactive personality on outcomes depends on the context and environment, highlighting the importance of how personality traits interact with situational factors.

## Myth or Science?

- **People can accurately judge certain personality traits within seconds of meeting someone, even from a brief introduction or a photo.**
- **First impressions, known as "zero acquaintance" judgments, have measurable validity, especially for traits like extraversion, which are easier to perceive quickly.**
- **Quick, intuitive judgments are often as accurate as more deliberate evaluations, and this applies both in-person and online.**
- **Some traits, such as emotional stability, are harder to detect unless the situation brings out those traits (e.g., a nervous reaction reveals more about emotional stability).**
- **The concept of "thin slices" explains why employment interviewers often form opinions about candidates within the first two minutes, and these initial impressions have moderate validity.**
- **Despite the accuracy of quick judgments, it is important to remain open-minded, as first impressions do not capture the full complexity of a person's personality.**

## Personality and Situations

- **Personality traits can predict behavior, but the strength of this prediction depends on the situation.** Traits like conscientiousness and extraversion are generally effective across many environments, such as job performance and leadership emergence.
- **Situational context plays a significant role in shaping how personality traits are expressed.** Situations can be categorized (e.g., positive-negative, complex, typical, important, humorous), and these categories can influence both behavior and which personality traits are activated.
- **Two key theoretical frameworks explain the interaction between personality and situation:** Situation strength theory (how strong or weak a situation is in dictating behavior) and trait activation theory (how certain situations trigger the expression of specific traits) are essential for understanding how context affects the relationship between personality and behavior in the workplace.

## Situation Strength Theory

- **Situation strength theory explains that the influence of personality on behavior depends on how strong or weak a situation is.** In strong situations, clear norms and expectations dictate behavior, limiting the expression of individual personality. In weak situations, there are fewer constraints, so personality traits are more likely to shape behavior.
    
- **Strong situations are characterized by four main elements:**
    
    - **Clarity:** Clear cues about duties and responsibilities (e.g., factory worker roles are clearer than those of a Hollywood agent).
    - **Consistency:** Compatible cues that point toward the same behavior (e.g., acute care nurse roles are more consistent than manager roles).
    - **Constraints:** Limited freedom due to external forces (e.g., bank examiners face more constraints than forest rangers).
    - **Consequences:** High stakes for decisions or actions (e.g., surgeons face greater consequences than language teachers).
- **Organizations often create strong situations through rules, norms, and standards, which can be beneficial for safety and ethical behavior but may also have drawbacks.** Strong situations can reduce undesirable behaviors (like carelessness or rule-breaking) by promoting compliance, but they may also make jobs feel monotonous and suppress creativity and innovation. The perception of situation strength by employees influences how they respond, and not all employees react the same way to strong or weak situations.
    

## Trait Activation Theory

- **Trait Activation Theory (TAT) explains that certain situations or events can "activate" specific personality traits, making them more influential in predicting behavior.** For example, commission-based jobs activate extraversion, while creative jobs activate openness.
- **TAT helps match personality traits to job suitability.** High scores on certain traits (like extraversion or openness) predict better performance in jobs where those traits are relevant, as indicated by plus (+) or minus (−) signs in job-trait matching.
- **Environmental features can trigger personality tendencies, impacting outcomes.** For instance, electronic monitoring activates fear of failure, reducing learning, while supportive coworkers activate conscientiousness, increasing information sharing.
- **Personality and situation interact to influence behavior.** Theories like situation strength and TAT show that both nature (personality) and nurture (situation) work together, and the right situation can amplify the predictive power of personality traits on behavior.

## Ability

- **Ability refers to an individual’s current capacity to perform various tasks in a job**, and is a key factor in organizational behavior.
- **Abilities are divided into two main categories: intellectual and physical**, each influencing workplace behavior and outcomes differently.
- **Intellectual abilities** involve mental activities such as thinking, reasoning, and problem-solving, which are crucial for jobs requiring cognitive skills.
- **Physical abilities** pertain to tasks requiring stamina, dexterity, strength, and similar attributes, and are essential for roles with physical demands.
- **Recognizing individual strengths and weaknesses in these abilities helps management assign roles more effectively** and increases the likelihood of job success.
- **Employees should market and develop their abilities** to align with the requirements of desired jobs, enhancing their chances of success.

## Intellectual Abilities

- **Intellectual abilities** are your capacity for mental activities like thinking, reasoning, and problem solving, and are measured through structured assessments as a form of cognitive capacity.
- **Intelligence** refers to measurable cognitive abilities, while being "smart" can include practical or social skills, and being an "intellectual" means engaging in abstract or theoretical thinking.
- Higher intelligence (as measured by IQ) is associated with better job performance, higher earnings, more promotions, and higher-level jobs, especially in complex roles, but does not necessarily lead to greater job satisfaction or happiness.
- People with high cognitive ability may experience negative social consequences at work, such as envy or mistreatment from peers.
- **Measuring intellectual ability** is complex; IQ tests and standardized tests like the SAT, ACT, GMAT, LSAT, and MCAT are controversial but are considered by experts to assess intelligence.
- Many organizations use intelligence tests in hiring, but these tests should be used carefully to avoid excluding underrepresented or protected groups.
- The **seven main dimensions of intellectual ability** are: number aptitude, verbal comprehension, perceptual speed, inductive reasoning, deductive reasoning, spatial visualization, and memory. These dimensions are positively correlated, supporting the concept of **general mental ability (GMA)**.
- GMA is recognized as a universal phenomenon across cultures, found in the majority of countries studied.
- The **Wonderlic Ability Test** is a widely used intelligence test in hiring, consisting of 50 questions in 12 minutes, measuring both speed and power, and is valued for its efficiency and validity.
- Intelligence tests are typically used alongside other hiring tools, not as replacements, and require careful monitoring to prevent disparate impact in HR practices.

## Physical Abilities

- **Physical abilities remain essential for many jobs, with nine basic abilities identified:** dynamic strength, trunk strength, static strength, explosive strength, extent flexibility, dynamic flexibility, body coordination, balance, and stamina.
- **Job performance is highest when employee abilities match job requirements;** for example, physical ability tests can predict performance in physically demanding roles like firefighting.
- **Physical ability tests may lead to discrimination, as men often score higher than women,** but physical fitness training can help individuals meet minimum qualifications; organizations should use ability measures carefully and prioritize diversity, ensuring assessments are relevant to job requirements.
- **Values are another key individual difference, representing belief systems rather than behavioral tendencies.**

## Values

- **Values are stable, enduring beliefs about what is morally, socially, or personally preferable**, and they include both what is important (content) and how important it is (intensity).
- **Your value system is a hierarchy of values** (such as freedom, honesty, equality) ranked by their relative importance, shaping your judgments of right and wrong and your preferences for behaviors and outcomes.
- **Values influence attitudes, motivation, and decision making** by forming the foundation for how you perceive situations, interpret events, and respond to organizational practices.
- **A mismatch between personal and organizational values can lead to dissatisfaction and reduced motivation**, as seen when individuals’ beliefs about fairness or reward systems conflict with company policies.
- **Certain value orientations, like openness to change and self-transcendence, are linked to lower perceived stress and greater resilience at work**, suggesting that values affect not just attitudes but also well-being.
- **Values are shaped early in life by influences such as family, teachers, and peers, and are often reinforced over time**, though they can change if questioned.
- **Personality traits are connected to value preferences**; for example, openness is associated with valuing new ideas, while agreeableness is linked to valuing cooperation and caring for others.
- **Values can both enhance and hinder decision making**—they provide guidance but may also bias objectivity if they conflict with organizational norms or practices.

## Generational Values

- **Generational labels like baby boomers, Gen Xers, millennials, and Gen Zers are commonly used to describe groups in the U.S. workforce, but these classifications lack strong research support.**
- **Many generalizations about generational work values are overblown or incorrect; for example, research does not support claims that millennials are more altruistic or that baby boomers have a stronger work ethic than other generations.**
- **Studies show that the value placed on leisure has increased and work centrality has declined across generations, but entitlement levels do not significantly differ between generations.**
- **Despite weak evidence for actual generational value differences, people in the workplace often stereotype others based on generational categories, which can influence workplace decisions and contribute to ageist or discriminatory environments.**

## Moral Foundations Theory

- **Moral Foundations Theory (MFT) identifies innate psychological systems that form the basis of human morality, explaining both universal themes and cultural differences in moral beliefs.**
- MFT originally proposed six key moral foundations: care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity, and liberty, each evolving from adaptive challenges faced by humans (e.g., protecting children, fostering cooperation).
- Each foundation is triggered by specific events that elicit key emotions and lead to the development of related virtues (e.g., generosity triggers gratitude, leading to reciprocity).
- The fairness foundation has been refined into two distinct concepts: equality (focus on equal treatment and outcomes) and proportionality (focus on rewards matching merit or contribution); the liberty foundation has been removed, and sanctity has been renamed purity.
- MFT does not prescribe which moral values people should have but describes the moral values that people actually possess, shaped by cultural narratives and institutions.
- In the workplace, MFT helps explain why employees may perceive leaders’ decisions differently based on their own moral foundations, highlighting the importance for leaders to align their actions with the moral codes of both employees and the organization.
- Followers’ moral foundations can significantly influence their reactions to leader behavior, as seen in cases where actions are interpreted through the lens of equality or other moral concerns.
