### Background

- Elizabeth Holmes founded Theranos in 2003 at age 19, dropping out of Stanford after her sophomore year (studying chemical engineering).
- Her big idea was a device that could run hundreds of medical tests from just a few drops of finger prick blood, instead of needing vials drawn from a vein.
- She named the device the Edison.
- She idolized Steve Jobs, reportedly copying his style, black turtlenecks, even his exact mannerisms and low speaking voice.

### The Pitch and the Hype

- Theranos claimed its technology could detect things like cancer, diabetes, and cholesterol issues from a tiny blood sample, cheaper and faster than traditional labs.
- At its peak, Theranos was valued around 9 billion dollars, making Holmes the youngest self made female billionaire on paper.
- She built a board stacked with big names, including former Secretary of State Henry Kissinger, former Defense Secretary James Mattis, and several senators, but almost nobody on the board had a medical or scientific background.
- Partnerships were formed with Walgreens and Safeway to roll out Theranos testing centers in stores.

### The Problem

- The technology never actually worked as promised. The Edison machine was unreliable and often gave wildly inaccurate results.
- Behind the scenes, Theranos was secretly using traditional third party machines (like ones from Siemens) to run most tests, while telling investors and the public their own tech was doing the work.
- Employees who raised concerns were pressured, isolated, or fired. There was a strong culture of secrecy, with departments walled off from each other so no one could see the full picture.
- Faulty results led to real patient harm, wrong diagnoses, unnecessary treatments, and dangerous false readings.

### The Unraveling

- Journalist John Carreyrou at The Wall Street Journal broke the story open in 2015 after being tipped off by former employees and a pathologist named Adam Clapper who blogged about the shady numbers.
- Key whistleblowers included Tyler Shultz (grandson of board member George Shultz) and Erika Cheung, both young Theranos employees who saw the fraud firsthand and risked everything to speak up.
- Holmes and her partner (both business and romantic) Sunny Balwani aggressively tried to silence critics using lawyers and NDAs.
- The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) and the SEC eventually investigated. Theranos voided or corrected hundreds of thousands of test results.

### Legal Fallout

- In 2018, the SEC charged Holmes with massive fraud. She settled by paying a fine, giving up control of the company, and being banned from serving as an officer or director of a public company for 10 years.
- Theranos dissolved in 2018.
- Criminal charges followed. Holmes and Balwani were tried separately for wire fraud and conspiracy.
- In January 2022, Holmes was convicted on 4 counts of defrauding investors (found not guilty on charges related to defrauding patients).
- She was sentenced in November 2022 to over 11 years in prison.
- Balwani was also convicted on all 12 counts against him and got an even longer sentence.
- Holmes reported to prison in 2023. She has an ongoing appeal.

### Her Defense

- At trial, Holmes testified she genuinely believed the technology would eventually work.
- She also made allegations that Balwani was emotionally and psychologically abusive toward her during their relationship, which she said affected her judgment and mental state during the years the fraud occurred. Balwani denied this.

### The TED Talk (2014)

- Delivered while Theranos was still riding high, before the Wall Street Journal exposé.
- In it, Holmes talks about her mission to make lab testing cheap, painless, and accessible, framing it as a personal cause tied to a fear of needles.
- She speaks in her famous deep voice, calm and controlled, which later became a huge point of discussion since former employees said the voice was an affectation, not natural.
- Watching it now is jarring in hindsight, since she confidently describes technology that internal employees already knew was failing basic accuracy tests.
- It's often cited as a case study in how charisma, confidence, and a good narrative can override actual scrutiny of the science.

### Big Themes to Take Away

- Fake it till you make it culture in Silicon Valley taken to a dangerous extreme, since this wasn't software, it was medical testing with real health consequences.
- The power of a compelling founder story (young, dropout, saving lives) to attract huge money and famous names without real technical due diligence.
- How secrecy and compartmentalization inside a company can hide fraud from almost everyone, including senior staff.
- The courage of the whistleblowers, especially young, low level employees like Cheung and Shultz, who had the most to lose.
- The gap between the myth of Holmes as the next Steve Jobs and the reality of a product that endangered patients.
